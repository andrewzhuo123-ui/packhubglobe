Shipping & protection

How do I investigate popped seals after a parcel arrives?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can find a parcel open at receipt and lose the evidence when someone pushes the tape back down to secure it.

I photograph the parcel as received and compare the tape, board, flaps and contents. I use the pattern to select a likely cause, then reproduce the original packing method before testing a correction.

I record what arrived before I decide why it happened. Different closure failures can look alike once the flap has been opened further.

What should I photograph before handling the parcel?

A photo of a loose tape end can leave me guessing which seam opened and whether the carton was crushed beside it.

I photograph the whole parcel and the affected seam, including the tape ends and nearby corners. I record which side was open and what handling occurred after receipt.

I preserve the condition the receiver found

I ask for a wide photo that shows the orientation of the parcel and its affected face. Then I ask for closer views of both ends of the seam. I include the tape edge, exposed board and any bend in the flap. If the parcel needs to be secured immediately, I take the photographs before that step where it is practical. I record any temporary closure so a later reviewer can distinguish it from the original one.

I identify the parcel and the carton revision if the record is available. I note when it arrived and who first saw the opening. I keep a picture of the shipping label for the internal record and mask personal information when I share the inspection with a wider group. The warehouse damage inspection guide helps me keep the wide views, close views and contents in a consistent sequence.

I photograph any dent or tear near the seam. A crushed corner can change the way the flaps meet. I also look for an opening on another side that might have changed the pressure inside the carton. If more than one parcel is affected, I record them individually before comparing patterns. I keep samples from the same order together without assuming they all failed for the same reason.

What can the tape and board tell me?

A loose strip can mean the adhesive released. It can also mean the tape held while a layer of board came away.

I compare the tape underside with the exposed carton surface. I look for transferred fibers, a clean release, a torn strip or a flap that has changed shape.

I treat each mark as a clue

What I see What I check next
Tape lifts with little board attached Surface, application and the exact tape used
Board fibers remain on the tape Surface damage and loads on the seam
Tape split across the joint Flap movement, a cut or nearby impact
One flap rises above the other Pack height, closure sequence and distortion

I look at both sides of the seam before pulling more tape away. If fibers are stuck to the tape, I check where they came from and whether the flap surface tore. If the strip looks clean, I ask how the carton was taped and what condition the board was in. I avoid calling either observation proof of the cause. Handling can change the condition after the first failure.

I identify the tape by its actual product reference. A manufacturer such as 3M publishes data for specific box sealing tapes.1 I use the document that applies to the material on the order and ask the supplier how the carton surface and application conditions affect its use. I also compare the tape pattern with the original instruction. FedEx's packing guidance includes an H taping method.2 I check the applicable carrier requirements and separate any missed application step from a possible material issue.

How do I check whether the load pushed the seam open?

A tape edge can lift because a product or pad is holding the flaps apart. I inspect the inside before moving the load.

I open the parcel in its received orientation and photograph the load. I compare high points, shifted supports and marks under the lid with the position of the opening.

I trace the pressure from the contents to the flap

I look for a product sitting above its intended support or a top pad that has moved. I check whether paperwork is folded into a thick bundle under the seam. I photograph the positions before lifting the products out. If a raised item sits under the highest part of the flap, I compare those locations and inspect the product for a matching pressure mark. I also check whether the item could have shifted only after the seam opened.

I rebuild the pack with the same parts and close it in the normal sequence. I check whether the flaps meet without forcing them down. I use the guide to inside and outside dimensions to record the usable room and the loaded carton dimensions. If the package bows before taping, I investigate the load and carton fit. A stronger tape may hold the flap while leaving the product under pressure.

I compare an empty sample with the loaded one and note where their shapes differ. If both show the same distortion, I check the carton and its forming sequence. If only the loaded pack shows the problem, I inspect the placement of the product and protective parts. I keep those observations with the original receipt photos.

How do I verify the correction before shipping more orders?

A fresh strip can keep one carton shut on the bench. I check that the correction works on the intended load and closure process.

I change the suspected cause, compare it with the original method, and inspect representative parcels after the relevant handling. I document which carton, tape and packing sequence passed.

I test the change against the observed defect

I select the smallest change that addresses the evidence. If the tape end lifted from a dusty face, I check the application process on the same board. If the contents held a flap up, I change the support or carton fit before I compare tapes. If a corner was damaged, I review the full shipping configuration and ask what kind of handling test represents the route. I use the mailer and shipping box guide if the outer format needs review.

I compare samples with the same product and record the closure condition at the bench and after the agreed handling. I check for renewed tape lift, board damage and any strain on the flaps. I also inspect the contents so a closed seam cannot hide a new pressure mark. I record the cases that pass and the ones that still need investigation.

I update the instruction only after the corrected version meets the agreed checks. I show the correct closure pattern and define visible defects that require a hold. I give receiving the same inspection points for the next shipment. If an opening recurs, the team can compare it with the previous photographs and the exact packing method.

Conclusion

I preserve the received condition, trace the likely failure through the closure and load, and verify the correction before using it on more orders.
