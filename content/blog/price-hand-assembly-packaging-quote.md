# How should I price the cost of hand assembly in a packaging quote?

I can price the box and insert correctly and still miss the labor needed to build a finished pack on every shift.

**I time the agreed work, multiply the labor hours by the relevant hourly cost, and spread setup and rework across the usable output. I ask each supplier to quote the same finished state and report what its price includes.**

I start by defining where the job begins and ends. Then I can tell whether two offers include the same work.

## Which assembly steps should I include in the quote?

One supplier may quote a formed box. Another may include the insert, ribbon and final inspection. I ask both to show the work.

**I list each task from opening the supply cases to putting a usable pack in the agreed handoff position. I separate assembly, product loading and final packing when different teams perform them.**

### I define the handoff with an actual sample

I lay out the parts as they arrive. For a gift box, the work may include opening a flat shell, peeling adhesive liners, seating the insert and attaching a ribbon. I also check whether the operator removes protective sheets and clears waste. I define the point where the next team takes over. If the quote covers an empty formed box, I do not add product loading to that supplier's assembly line item. I still account for loading elsewhere if the buying decision depends on it.

I use the [packaging quote checklist](/guides/packaging-quote-checklist/) to keep materials, tooling, assembly and freight visible. I ask the supplier to state whether staging and inspection are included. If the box needs time to set after assembly, I distinguish the operator's time from the space or delay needed before the next step. I ask who provides any adhesive, fixture or protective material.

I review the sequence with the people who will do the work. A sample maker may reach for tools that a production operator does not have. I ask for a simple work instruction that shows the order of operations and the expected result. That gives each supplier a common basis for quoting and gives me something to check during the trial.

## How do I measure labor when several people share the work?

A station can finish one pack quickly while two people work at once. I measure total person-time as well as station output.

**I observe a representative run and sum the time each operator spends on the work. I record elapsed time separately so I can compare labor cost and packing capacity.**

### I time the work at normal pace

I check that the operators have normal tools, parts and space. I let them learn the sequence before I use the run to estimate routine work. Then I time a representative group of units. I note interruptions, missing parts and restocking. I include recurring tasks in the estimate and keep exceptional disruptions visible. The Lean Enterprise Institute's description of standardized work includes work sequence, timing and inventory.[^1] I record the actual sequence so another person can understand why the time changed.

| Illustrative timing | Person-time per unit |
| --- | ---: |
| Operator A forms the box | 35 seconds |
| Operator B inserts the tray and checks it | 25 seconds |
| Total direct labor | 60 seconds |

If the two people work in parallel, the line may produce a finished box faster than one per minute. The direct labor still totals one person-minute per box on these assumptions. I use that distinction when I compare paying for labor with meeting a dispatch deadline. I also record how many acceptable boxes come out over the observed period.

I use the [packaging sample types guide](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to make sure the parts represent the intended process. A hand-cut insert that needs extra trimming can make a production job look slower. A demonstration using already formed parts can make it look faster. I identify both differences before I extrapolate the result.

## How do I turn the observed work into a unit price?

A direct labor figure can miss setup and correction. I keep those amounts visible instead of hiding them in a guessed rate.

**I add direct person-hours, batch setup and expected rework using a consistent rate. I divide the total by accepted units and state which costs the rate already includes.**

### I separate batch costs from per-unit work

I ask the business or supplier which hourly rate it uses for this comparison. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics separates wages and benefit costs in its employer compensation reporting.[^2] I use the rate for my actual operation and document which costs it includes. If a supplier's unit charge already includes overhead and margin, I avoid adding those charges to the same work again. I compare either complete quotes or consistently defined cost components.

For an illustrative order, I assume 600 pieces start assembly and each takes one person-minute. That is ten person-hours. I add one hour for batch setup and half an hour for observed correction work. At an illustrative $24 per hour, the labor cost is $276. If 590 units are accepted, the labor cost is about $0.47 per usable unit. I keep replacement material and any separate handling charge visible when they apply. I do not assume the example's yield represents my supplier.

I run the calculation at the order sizes I expect to buy. The same setup cost adds more per piece to a smaller batch. I also check whether a larger run changes staffing, workstations or output. I record those assumptions beside the price so the buyer can update the comparison when the order quantity changes.

## How do I compare supplier assembly with doing it in-house?

A low supplier charge can arrive with more freight or less flexibility. In-house work can become expensive when the packing bench is already full.

**I compare each option at the same ready-to-use state. I include any change in freight, storage and scheduling, then verify which team can meet the quality and delivery requirements.**

### I check the effect on the whole packing operation

I ask for two versions of the quote if both routes are practical. I confirm how the parts will be delivered and what protection is needed in transit. Assembled units may use more space. Flat parts may need extra handling and staging. I use the [landed cost comparison guide](/blog/compare-packaging-quotes-landed-cost/) to compare delivery costs, then add whichever assembly and inspection work remains at my site.

I compare output during normal and peak periods. A small saving per box can matter on a steady order. The same arrangement may create a bottleneck on a short launch if the station has to work on several products at once. I ask which deadline the team can support and whether it needs a second shift or extra space. I put those costs in the relevant scenario.

I keep the agreed work sequence, labor assumptions and accepted sample with the purchase record. If the supplier changes the insert, closure or way it ships the parts, I check whether the work has changed. I compare actual assembly time and usable output on the first order with the estimate. I use that evidence for the next quote. I also check whether the supplier can ship smaller releases at the agreed price. That matters when I can store flat parts for a full order but only have room for a week of assembled boxes.

## Conclusion

I price hand assembly from measured person-time and accepted output, then compare both options at the same finished state.

[^1]: [Lean Enterprise Institute on standardized work](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/standardized-work/) describes timing and work sequence in production.
[^2]: [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics on employer costs for wages and benefits](https://www.bls.gov/blog/2023/tracking-employer-costs-for-wages-and-benefits.htm) distinguishes the main elements of employer compensation.
