# How do I design a mailer for both single items and bundles?

I can save a shelf slot by buying one mailer, then spend the difference filling empty space in every single-item order.

**I design one mailer around two tested packing recipes. I check that the single item stays in place and the full bundle closes without strain. Then I compare packing time, materials and shipping cost against using two box sizes.**

I start with real order combinations. The best shared carton is the one the packing team can use reliably for both loads.

## Which order combinations should I design around?

A box that fits a bundle on paper can still fail on the bench. I need space for the parts that keep the products safe.

**I choose the most common single-item order and the largest bundle I plan to ship in the same mailer. I also check any smaller item or unusual shape that could move more inside the box.**

### I define the two loads before measuring the carton

I make an item list for each recipe. For a single jar I might include the jar, its retail carton, an insert and a receipt. For a three-item gift order I might include two jars and a flat accessory. I put the actual parts on the bench, including dividers and wrapping. A bundle described as three products can mean three identical boxes or three very different shapes. Those loads may use the available space differently.

I measure what the protective parts need as well as what the products need. I check how far the insert projects below or above the product. I measure the gap needed to put the product in without rubbing a printed surface. I also check the room I need to remove it. The [guide to inside and outside box dimensions](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) helps me describe the clear space separately from the dimensions that appear on a carrier quote.

I write down which orders are outside the plan. A tall refill bottle may share the same catalog family but sit higher than the lid will allow. A heavy gift set might need a different outer carton even though it fits inside the proposed mailer. I give the packing team a clear route for those cases. I can then test two defined recipes without accidentally promising that every combination in the catalog can ship in one box.

## How do I stop a single item moving in the larger mailer?

A loose item can strike the wall or lid during handling. Extra material helps only if it stays where the packer put it.

**I use a fitted part or a repeatable cushioning layout to hold the item in its intended position. I check movement in each direction and make sure the lid does not press on the product.**

### I check what holds the item in each direction

I test movement along the length, width and depth. A folded pad might stop side-to-side motion while leaving the product free to lift toward the lid. I look for witness marks on the product or its retail box after handling the sample. If I see rubbing near a corner, I identify the surface it contacted before adding more material. A rough bench check helps me diagnose a layout problem. I use a suitable test plan to assess performance through the delivery route.

FedEx's packing guide recommends filling void spaces to help prevent movement.[^1] I translate that advice into a specific recipe for the warehouse. For example, I might use one folded paperboard support that locks into the ends of the mailer, rather than ask the packer to judge a handful of paper. I check that the support goes in one way, stays seated after closure, and is easy to remove during opening. I also check that it leaves a clear place for any leaflet.

I compare the recipe with a compact single-item box. I count the extra parts, note whether the packer has to form them by hand, and watch where a new packer hesitates. If the solution depends on a hard-to-repeat fold or exact placement of loose fill, I simplify it. The design should produce a similar packed result across normal shifts.

## How do I check that a full bundle still closes correctly?

A bundle may fit before the lid comes down. The lid can push a divider out of place or compress a product after the last flap closes.

**I close the fullest bundle using the normal packing method. I check for product contact, divider displacement, lid bulge and weak closure, then test the complete shipping configuration.**

### I watch how the load changes during closing

I arrange the parts in the order the packer will handle them. If the tallest item sits under a tuck flap, I check whether that flap clears it before closing. I look for pressure on closures, pumps or display windows. When I open the carton again, I check if the dividers still stand where they began. I repeat the trial with representative samples when product dimensions can vary. A result from one unusually small item gives me little information about the next delivery.

I compare the outer dimensions of the two closed recipes. The nominal box size may be identical, but a loaded lid can bow. I note where the packing team would measure the parcel and I keep any pack that needs extra force or tape separate for review. If I need a heavier shipping format, I check the [comparison of mailer and shipping boxes](/guides/mailer-boxes-vs-shipping-boxes/) before asking the supplier to reinforce the current design.

I use a transit plan that represents both recipes. ISTA lists Procedure 3A for eligible individual packages in parcel delivery.[^2] I ask the test provider which procedure and acceptance criteria suit the actual route and product. I bring a complete single load and a complete bundle load. One passing load does not tell me how the other behaves. I record the carton, insert, product mix and closure for each result so a later change can be assessed against what was tested.

## When should I keep two mailer sizes?

One shared box simplifies reordering. It can add work and cost to the order I ship most often.

**I keep two sizes when a smaller box materially reduces recurring packing or shipping costs, or when a shared box cannot protect both loads. I compare those costs with stocking the second size.**

### I use order volume to weight the comparison

I compare the cost per fulfilled order for both recipes. I include the carton, insert, cushioning, measured packing time and the shipping price for the packed configuration. Then I multiply each difference by the number of orders I actually expect for that recipe. I record the date and period for the forecast. A holiday mix with more bundles can give a different result from the rest of the year.

| Cost or constraint | One shared mailer | Two mailer sizes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Box stock | One main reorder line | Separate counts and reorder points |
| Single-item packing | May need a fitted spacer | May need fewer parts |
| Bundle packing | Must fit the largest planned load | Can use a box built for the load |
| Shipping quote | Uses packed dimensions for each recipe | Uses the corresponding packed size |

I use the [guide to mailer size and shipping cost](/blog/right-size-mailer-box-shipping-cost/) to compare the measured parcels. In a simple example, an extra $0.22 on each of 800 single-item orders adds $176. I compare that with the storage and buying cost of a compact second carton over the same period. I keep any damage cost separate unless I have real shipment data. I record the threshold where the choice changes so I can revisit the decision after the order mix shifts.

I also observe what happens when either box runs low. If the team uses a larger box as a substitute, I give it a tested single-item recipe for that case. I write the acceptable product combinations on the packing instructions and train the team using the actual samples.

## Conclusion

I share a mailer when both recipes protect the load and make financial sense. I keep two sizes when the order mix or transit results favor them.

[^2]: [ISTA test procedures](https://ista.org/test_procedures.php?test=3A) lists the scope of Procedure 3A and other distribution tests.
