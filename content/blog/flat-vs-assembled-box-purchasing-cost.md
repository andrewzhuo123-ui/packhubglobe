# Which costs change if I buy boxes flat rather than assembled?

I can save on inbound freight by buying boxes flat and then find that forming them slows down every order I ship.

**I compare the delivered cost, storage space, assembly time and usable yield for both versions. I choose the format that meets the finished box requirements at the lower total cost for my order volume and packing operation.**

I start with the same finished box requirement. Then I follow each option from the supplier's shipment to the point where my team can use it.

## What am I comparing when a supplier says the box ships flat?

A flat carton can arrive glued and ready to open, or it can arrive as a blank that needs several assembly steps.

**I ask how each box arrives and what I must do to make it usable. I compare the formed dimensions, closure, appearance and product fit before treating the quotes as equivalent.**

### I separate the shipping state from the finished structure

I ask the supplier to send pictures of the box as delivered and after forming. A folding carton might arrive with a glued seam and require only opening and closing. A presentation box might need adhesive tabs pressed down, a ribbon fitted, and an insert placed. I list those steps before I compare prices. I also ask which tools, supplies and work surfaces the packer needs. A box that forms without equipment may still need a large clean area for staging.

FEFCO's code provides a way to identify corrugated structures by design.[^1] I use a common style reference when the options are corrugated. I still ask the supplier to describe the delivery state. I also use the [folding carton and rigid box guide](/guides/folding-cartons-vs-rigid-boxes/) when the supplier proposes a different construction. I do not compare an assembled rigid box with a flat collapsible one without checking whether the finished presentation is acceptable.

I request a representative sample of each delivery condition. I build and load the flat version using the proposed instructions, then compare it with the assembled box. I inspect corners, lid alignment and the fit of the insert. If one option cannot meet the appearance or fit requirement, I ask for a revised quote before comparing its cost.

## How do freight and storage change with the delivery format?

A lower box price can hide the cost of shipping and storing air. I ask for the dimensions of the bundles and shipping cases.

**I request the packed case dimensions, quantities and weights for each option. I compare freight quotes and storage positions for the same usable delivery quantity.**

### I count the packages I will actually receive

I ask how many boxes fit in a case and how the cases fit on a pallet if the shipment uses pallets. I include the shipping materials and any parts delivered separately. A stack of flat shells may take little room while the formed inserts need their own bulky shipment. I check the combined delivery and ask whether the supplier will send both parts together. I use the [packaging quote checklist](/guides/packaging-quote-checklist/) so the two quotes carry the same delivery terms.

For a parcel shipment, I ask the carrier for a quote based on the actual packed size and weight. FedEx explains how dimensional weight can affect rating.[^2] I check the applicable service instead of applying a parcel rule to a pallet shipment. For a pallet delivery, I use the quoted configuration and ask about the charge basis for that route. I include receipt handling when one option takes substantially more time to unload or move.

I also check storage at my site. I compare the pallet or shelf positions needed for each delivery, then add the space required for the boxes staged at the packing bench. If I form flat boxes ahead of time, they may occupy the same space as assembled boxes during a busy week. I estimate how long I will keep that stock ready to use.

## How do I measure the labor and waste from assembly?

A quick trial by an experienced packer can understate the work. I measure the full sequence with representative people and materials.

**I time unpacking, forming, adding parts and setting the box aside ready to fill. I record rework and rejected boxes separately so the comparison reflects usable output.**

### I observe the actual work at the bench

I ask the team to form a batch using the instructions it will have during normal work. I include opening the supply case, removing protective material and placing the formed boxes in reach of the next operation. I record tasks the assembled option still requires. It might need a protective sleeve removed or an insert fitted. I compare the difference in time rather than charging all handling to one option.

| Illustrative extra work for the flat option | Calculation |
| --- | --- |
| Additional time per box | 25 seconds |
| Quantity formed | 1,200 boxes |
| Additional labor time | About 8.3 hours |
| Cost at an illustrative loaded rate of $24 per hour | $200 |

I replace those figures with observed times and the rate the business uses for planning. I also check when the hours occur. An extra hour before dispatch can matter more than an hour when the team has spare capacity. If the team forms boxes in advance, I check the storage and handling required to move them to the packing line.

I inspect the output for misaligned corners, weak joints and damage from assembly. I count reworked pieces and those the team cannot use. I check whether the supplier can simplify an awkward step. If the improvement changes the box design, I request a fresh sample and repeat the affected part of the trial.

## How do I choose between the two quotes?

A flat option may be cheaper at steady volume and harder to run during a peak week. I compare costs and capacity at the same time.

**I divide the total cost for each option by the boxes I expect to use. I compare the result at normal and peak volume and confirm the team can supply formed boxes on time.**

### I keep each cost visible in the decision

I add the box price, inbound freight, storage, assembly labor and relevant consumables. I account for rejects and replacements without counting the same loss twice. I use the [landed cost comparison guide](/blog/compare-packaging-quotes-landed-cost/) to align the delivery assumptions, then add the work needed to bring each option to the same ready-to-fill state. I record the volume and period so the comparison can be updated.

I look for the point where a change in volume reverses the decision. If the flat option saves $300 on the delivery but adds $200 in labor, I check what remains after the other differences. I ask whether the time saving from assembled boxes would let the team dispatch more orders during a short peak. If I need temporary help or another workstation for the flat option, I include that cost in the peak scenario.

I record the chosen delivery state in the order and retain a sample. I specify the included inserts and any assembly instructions. If the supplier changes the way it packs or forms the boxes on a later order, I review the affected costs and repeat the fit check. This makes the decision traceable to the actual boxes the team received.

## Conclusion

I compare both options at the ready-to-fill stage, using actual delivery dimensions, measured assembly time and the number of boxes I can use.

[^2]: [FedEx dimensional weight guidance](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packaging/what-is-dimensional-weight.html) explains how packed dimensions can affect parcel rating.
