How do I check a recycled-content claim against the exact material?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

A supplier can send me a recycled-content certificate for a material it sells. My packaging might use a different grade from the one listed.

I match the evidence to the exact grade, component and production lot. I check what the percentage measures and which parts the wording covers. I then verify that the purchase specification and delivered material support the same claim.

I begin with the words I want to print. Those words tell me what I need to prove and where a general supplier statement may leave a gap.

Which part does my claim actually describe?

A percentage beside a logo can sound like it describes the whole pack. The evidence may cover only the board.

I identify the exact component, material and percentage covered by the evidence. I compare that scope with what a buyer would understand from the text and its placement.

I name the component before adding a percentage

I use separate names for the outer carton, protective insert, clear window, label and any separate bag. If a supplier declaration covers a particular board grade, I attach it to the board record. I check whether that record covers the finished printed carton or only the incoming board. I use the packaging materials guide to align terminology with the supplier, then record the trade name and specification code for the material I will buy.

I compare possible wording against the pack layout. A statement about the board can be clear when it names the board directly. A large percentage on the front, with the named component tucked into tiny text on the bottom, can be read differently. I put the scope next to the percentage. I ask a colleague unfamiliar with the pack to point to the part the statement describes. If they point to the whole pack, I revise the wording or its placement.

I separate statements about recycled inputs from instructions about what to do after use. The FTC's Green Guides summary addresses those as separate claim types.1 I check the intended market before approving the artwork. A recycled-input declaration answers a different question from whether a local program accepts the finished package.

How do I verify the supplier's evidence matches my order?

A certificate can be real and still describe a different site or grade. I follow the material code from the document through the purchase record.

I check the issuer, scope, grade, facility and applicable dates. I ask the supplier to link the evidence to the material on my order and explain how it identifies qualifying lots.

I look for a traceable chain of references

I start with a current technical specification and the supplier's signed statement or verification record. I compare the full grade code, including suffixes. I check the facility and product family where the document names them. If the document shows a range of grades, I ask the supplier to identify the row covering my exact material. I also ask whether the figure is a guaranteed minimum, an average, or a result for a particular lot. That distinction matters when the words on the artwork promise a fixed level.

I ask for the record that connects the qualifying material to the order. This might be a purchase line with the grade specified and a production or delivery record with the relevant lot identifier. I retain whichever evidence the supplier can provide and check that a second person can follow the references. When a distributor uses its own product code, I ask for the correspondence between that code and the producer's grade.

UL Solutions distinguishes methods used to validate recycled content.2 I read the scope before relying on a validation. I check whether it supports the claim I plan to make and whether it refers to physical content or to an allocation method. If I cannot reconcile the method with the wording, I keep the claim on hold while the supplier and market reviewer clarify the evidence.

How do I check the percentage on a finished package?

A supplier's percentage for board cannot simply be copied onto a statement about the complete packaging set.

I match the numerator and denominator to the same scope. For a finished-pack weight claim, I use documented recycled mass and total finished pack mass, including components within that scope.

I work through the numbers with the actual bill of materials

I record each component's mass and the evidence supporting its recycled fraction. I use the same units throughout. If the supplier's percentage covers a particular layer of a laminate, I ask for the mass of that layer before using it in a calculation. I keep the original document beside the calculation and state how the components were measured.

Illustrative component Mass Documented recycled fraction Recycled mass counted
Carton board 40 g 60% 24 g
Separate tray 10 g No fraction counted in this example 0 g
Packaging set 50 g 48% on these assumptions 24 g

These are example figures. I would confirm whether other pieces, coatings or attachments fall inside the claim before doing this for a real pack. I would also check whether production variation affects the figure I can support. If the evidence only supports a claim for the board, I keep the published wording focused on that board. I retain the assumptions even when the percentage appears straightforward.

When I buy more than one size, I check the mass balance for each size. A tray might use the same weight while the board changes. The finished package percentage can then differ even though the board percentage stays the same. I use the quote checklist to request material and component details before the artwork is finalized.

How do I keep the claim accurate when I reorder?

A substitute can meet the same fit requirements and still carry different evidence. I make the claim review part of purchasing.

I put the approved material and evidence requirement on the order. I require notice before substitutions, check delivered lots against the record, and reassess the wording when the pack or supply changes.

I define the point at which the order must stop for review

I put the approved grade and claim scope in the material specification. I ask the supplier to tell me when it changes the source, grade or construction. I assign a person to review any replacement and decide whether the current artwork can still be used. A change request should carry enough information to compare the replacement with the existing pack. I ask for the new specification, evidence and expected production lot before I approve it.

I keep a copy of the approved artwork with the evidence and the order reference. At receipt, I compare the supplier's documentation with the purchase specification. If a lot arrives under a different code, I ask whether that code reflects a new material or a documented equivalent. I review the record before sending the package to filling or dispatch. I keep qualifying lots identifiable when the warehouse handles several revisions.

I check the printed statement again at the next artwork review. A change to a window or coating can affect what the finished component contains even when the same board remains in use. The guide to kraft packaging and coatings provides questions I can use to confirm the construction. I record the updated decision and retain the previous version for orders that already shipped.

Conclusion

I approve a recycled-content claim when the wording, material evidence, calculation and purchase record all describe the same packaging.
