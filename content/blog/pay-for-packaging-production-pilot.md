# When should I pay for a packaging production pilot?

I can approve a beautiful sample and still find that bulk cartons jam on the packing line. The sample never went through that line.

**I pay for a packaging production pilot when a machine, material or assembly risk remains after sample approval and a short representative run can change the buying decision. I agree on the evidence and bulk release conditions before paying.**

I begin with the failure I want to avoid. Then I check whether the supplier can reproduce the conditions under which that failure might appear.

## Which problems justify paying for a pilot?

A more expensive sample does not automatically answer a better question. I look for a decision that depends on the actual production process.

**I ask for a pilot when I need to observe repeatability, production tooling, automated packing, or another condition a hand sample cannot show. I use a simpler sample for questions it can answer.**

### I name the uncertainty before requesting the quote

For example, I might have a carton that fits the product in a hand assembly trial. The unresolved question is whether the glue joint and crease allow the filled carton to close reliably on the intended line. I tell the converter which part of that process I need to see. If I only need to confirm where the logo sits, I ask for a suitable proof. I use the [packaging sample types guide](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to match the sample to the decision.

I also think about what happens if I find the fault during the bulk run. If correcting it requires changing the die or replacing already printed stock, a trial can give me a chance to make that decision earlier. I compare the cost and time of the trial with the specific correction I might otherwise face. I avoid assigning a failure probability I cannot support. I can still compare the known pilot fee with several plausible outcomes and state the assumptions.

I check that a pilot can reveal the problem. A short run can show whether a flap catches repeatedly on the line. It may provide little evidence about a rare defect. I ask the quality team what it would need to observe or measure before using the result to release the order.

## What should the supplier reproduce during the pilot?

A supplier may call a run a pilot while using a different board or machine. I ask for the conditions that matter to the decision.

**I specify the intended stock, tooling, equipment and packing sequence. I ask the supplier to list any differences from bulk production and explain how each difference affects the trial.**

### I put the comparison in the order record

| Trial input | What I ask the supplier to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Material | Actual grade, thickness and finish |
| Tooling | Tool reference and drawing revision |
| Equipment | Which line performs the relevant operation |
| Packing sequence | Actual product, insert and closure |
| Run records | Sample identities, settings and exceptions |

I ask which steps take place at the converter and which take place at the packer. If I need to check how cartons feed on a filling line, I arrange for representative pieces to reach that line. A successful converting trial cannot establish how the same pieces will behave at a separate packing plant. I agree who records the conditions and who can answer questions if the line stops.

I also ask the converter how it will select the pieces I see. I want a view of the relevant part of the run, including changes in output that may occur after setup. NIST's process characterization handbook distinguishes studying a production process from examining a single output.[^1] I ask the supplier to retain observations across the agreed trial instead of sending only the best looking pieces.

If a material or setup cannot match production, I narrow the decision accordingly. A pilot on alternative board might show how a tab is meant to engage. I would still need to confirm that operation on the production grade before relying on it for the full order.

## How do I set the pass and hold conditions?

A trial can end with a box of samples and no clear decision. I write down what will release the bulk order before the run starts.

**I agree on the checks, sample selection, acceptance limits and person who can release production. I define which defects require correction and another trial.**

### I write acceptance conditions that the team can observe

I start with the issue that justified the fee. If cartons are hard to close on a line, I record the intended packing setup and the type of defect that would require a hold. I ask the team to document stops, damaged flaps and incomplete closures. I also check the finished carton against the agreed dimensions and appearance. I separate the observations so a good print result cannot hide a repeated assembly problem.

If a transit trial is part of the purchase decision, I ask what complete pack and route the test will represent. ISTA's design and testing guidance discusses choosing a test to suit the packaged product and distribution setting.[^2] I keep that result separate from evidence that the converter produced acceptable cartons. I also state whether a defect observed after shipping requires a pack redesign or investigation before release.

I use the [print proof approval checklist](/blog/packaging-print-proof-approval-checklist/) for the artwork and finish checks that remain relevant. I name who approves each part and identify the version being reviewed. If one issue is unresolved, I record that issue and the required follow-up. I avoid giving the supplier a general approval that it could read as permission to start the full order.

## How do I compare the pilot fee with the buying decision?

A trial fee can be reasonable while the quote still leaves me exposed to repeat charges or unusable pieces.

**I compare the full trial cost with the decisions it could change. I confirm what the fee includes, whether any pieces can be used, and what happens if a correction requires another run.**

### I price the work that will actually happen

I ask the supplier to separate setup, material, output, testing, freight and any line time. I use the [packaging quote checklist](/guides/packaging-quote-checklist/) to keep the pilot and bulk amounts visible. I ask whether the fee is credited toward a later order and what conditions apply. I also ask who pays for a repeat trial if the output misses an agreed condition. The answer can change which quote makes sense even when both suppliers propose similar samples.

I ask what I can do with acceptable pieces. If they are production-ready and meet the agreed conditions, I can count them only after the quality team releases them. If they are marked for testing or made with a different material, I keep them separate from saleable stock. I check how many pieces need to be retained as evidence before deciding how many remain available.

I record what the pilot will let me approve, change, or stop. If the supplier can show a representative result and the answer could change the bulk order, I can justify paying for the information. If the result would leave the same decision unresolved, I change the trial scope before committing. Once the run is complete, I retain the findings and written release with the final specification.

## Conclusion

I pay for a pilot when representative production evidence can change my order decision, and I define the release conditions before the trial starts.

[^1]: [NIST Engineering Statistics Handbook on production process characterization](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/toolaids/pff/ppc.pdf) covers the study of manufacturing process output.
[^2]: [ISTA guidance on design and testing](https://ista.org/getting_started_with_design.php) discusses planning packaged-product testing for distribution.
