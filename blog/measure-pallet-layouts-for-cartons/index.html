Shipping & protection

Which measurements help me compare pallet layouts for cartons?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can fit more cases onto a drawing and still lose those gains when the loaded pallet will not fit through receiving.

I measure loaded cartons, usable deck space, overhang, finished height and gross weight. I compare the cases per layer and per pallet, then check how the secured loads hold their shape and meet receiving requirements.

I compare pallet layouts for cartons in two passes. First I check whether they fit. Then I build and measure each candidate as a complete load.

Which dimensions do I need before drawing a pattern?

A carton drawing can show a clean rectangle. The closed case may be wider where flaps overlap or contents push out a wall.

I measure the outside of several loaded cartons and the usable pallet deck. I record which carton orientations are allowed and check whether the proposed footprint leaves room for normal variation.

I work from filled cases and the actual pallet

I close the cases using the normal method and measure the longest and widest outside spans without pressing the sides flat. I check height too, including any point where the case has a raised flap. I use the inside and outside box dimensions guide to distinguish these readings from the dimensions the converter uses for product fit. If the cases come in different sizes, I measure and label each type. I check that the pattern uses those types in the intended orientation.

I measure the pallet that the supplier will actually use. I check its deck edges and note any part of the platform that cannot support the carton as intended. I also ask receiving for the maximum load envelope and any instructions for pallet entry or handling. A deck dimension alone does not tell me whether a secured load can travel through the next facility.

I mark the orientation restrictions before I draw the layer. A case may be allowed to turn on the deck but may need to stay upright. I ask the product and warehouse teams to confirm the rules for the actual order. I record those limits next to the dimensions so a pattern that only works with a case on its side is easy to rule out.

How do I compare layer count, gaps and overhang?

A layer with one more carton can leave an unsupported edge or an uneven outside face. I record the layout and its physical result.

I count the cases in each layer and measure the finished footprint on both axes. I check any overhang and unused gaps against the carrier and receiving requirements before comparing capacity.

I measure each edge of a trial layer

Measurement What I record
Cases per layer Count and orientation by row
Finished footprint Widest loaded span on each deck axis
Clearance or overhang Distance at each edge, with the location marked
Internal gaps Largest gaps that can let a case move

I build the first layer on the pallet and compare it with the drawing. I check whether the dimensions allow the team to place the final carton without forcing it. I measure the footprint after the cases settle. I note any edge that extends beyond the deck or a corner that sits partly unsupported. FedEx's pallet guidance discusses stacking and avoiding overhang.1 I use the requirements for the chosen freight service and receiving site when judging the pattern.

I also look for gaps inside the layer. If one case can move into the gap, I record where it can travel and whether the next layer changes the result. I photograph the trial layer and keep the placement order. I use the guide to selecting shipping boxes when a change to the carton structure or size would make the pattern more usable. I compare the original and revised cases with their actual contents so a dimensional change does not hide a packing problem.

What should I measure after the full pallet is built?

A good first layer can produce a tall or unstable load once the team repeats it. I measure the pallet after securing it.

I record total height, gross weight and the maximum secured footprint. I check leaning, edge alignment and carton deformation, including changes caused by the way the load is secured.

I check the load in its final condition

I build the planned number of layers and include the pallet, top cap and other parts that contribute to the height. I measure from the bottom of the pallet to the highest point of the secured load. I use the gross weight for the complete unit and check the applicable handling and equipment limits. I record whether the unit was weighed or calculated from component weights so the next team can repeat the check.

I look at the load from each side before and after securing it. I note if the securing method pulls a corner inward, bows a carton wall or shifts the top layer. I compare how the base supports the corners and whether the outside face stays aligned. I inspect the lower cartons for visible compression and record any change during the planned holding period. I check the condition again after ordinary movement by trained staff using the site's handling process.

I ask a test provider what evidence is needed if the load must be qualified for distribution. ISTA lists procedures for several distribution types, including unitized loads.2 I describe the actual load and route before choosing one. I use the bench measurements to compare layouts and retain any formal test results separately.

How do I choose between two layouts that both fit?

A pallet that holds more cartons can take longer to build or leave receiving with an awkward partial layer. I compare the full operation.

I compare usable cartons per pallet, build time, stability and receiving constraints. I record the approved pattern and check how it handles partial loads and normal carton variation.

I compare the actual order quantities

I calculate the total cartons per pallet using the verified layers. I also calculate the number of full and partial pallets for the order I plan to ship. A pattern that fits more cartons per layer may make little difference on a small order. I ask the receiving team how it handles a partial top layer and whether a different pattern is needed to secure it. I include any extra materials or handling in the comparison.

I use the landed cost comparison guide to compare freight on the same shipment terms. I also time the build and check whether the pattern is easy to repeat. I ask the team to assemble one trial from the written layout. If a row has to be rebuilt repeatedly, I clarify the placement or choose a simpler pattern that meets the same limits.

I save a top view and a side view with the case and pallet references. I include cases per layer, number of layers, final dimensions and gross weight. I identify what to check if the carton, quantity or pallet changes. That gives the warehouse an approved reference for the next shipment. I also record which side the receiving team must approach with its handling equipment. I check that the secured load leaves that entry accessible.

Conclusion

I choose the pallet layout that meets measured load limits, protects the cartons and can be built and received consistently.
