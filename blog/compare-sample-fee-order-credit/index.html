Sourcing & cost

How do I compare a sample fee with an order credit?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can pay for a sample expecting the fee back, then learn the credit applies only to a larger order than I need.

I compare the cash due for the sample with the total cost if I cancel, revise, or place the order. I count a credit only in scenarios where the order meets its written conditions.

I ask both suppliers to quote the same sample and shipment. Then I check when the fee is due and what the credit would actually reduce.

What exactly does the sample fee cover?

Two quotes can say custom sample and offer different work. I check what I will receive before comparing the prices.

I ask for the sample type, quantity, materials, print method, revision allowance and delivery terms. I keep the work charge and the cost of getting the samples to me separate.

I quote the same decision with each supplier

I start with what the sample must show. If I need to check product fit, I ask for a formed structural sample in suitable stock. If I also need to judge the color and finish, I specify those checks and ask how closely the sample matches production. I use the guide to packaging sample types to name the sample consistently. That avoids comparing a blank mockup with a printed sample as if they offered the same evidence.

I ask which setup work is included. The supplier may quote one sample but charge separately for a second size, a corrected print, or a different material. I ask how many copies the fee buys and who will receive them. If the designer and packer work at different sites, I include both shipments in the comparison. Alibaba's buyer guidance discusses asking suppliers about samples before ordering wholesale.1 I use the supplier's written response to confirm the exact terms for my project.

I ask for the courier price and what it covers. FedEx explains that parcel size can affect the rating weight.2 I check the packed dimensions when samples are large or assembled. A supplier that quotes a lower sample fee can still require more cash up front once shipping is included. I also check how long the quoted service takes, because a later arrival can reduce the time available to make corrections.

When can I actually use the order credit?

The words credit on order sound simple. I need to know which order, which date, and which charges qualify.

I confirm the minimum order, eligible items, expiry date and the invoice line the credit will reduce. I ask how it applies if the specification or order quantity changes.

I walk through an order I could really place

I give the supplier my likely order size and product mix. I ask it to show the credit against that order. If the threshold applies to each design, I check the individual lines. If the credit is limited to the exact sampled structure, I ask what happens when the insert changes during review. I also confirm whether the buyer must place the order within a set period after receiving the sample.

Credit term What I confirm
Eligible purchase Item, specification and minimum quantity
Eligible amount Which portion of the sample payment can be credited
Timing Deadline and invoice where the credit appears
Changes Treatment of a revised design, split order or second sample

I ask the supplier to distinguish a refund from an invoice credit. A refund returns money through the agreed payment process. A credit reduces an eligible amount due on a later purchase. I record the difference in my cash plan. If the supplier says freight is excluded, I keep that amount in every scenario. I use the packaging quote checklist to keep the sample, freight, credit and production price visible on separate lines.

I also ask whether multiple sample fees can be credited on the same order. If I need both a structure trial and a printed proof, I record each fee and its conditions. I save the supplier's reply beside the quote so someone reviewing the order can see how the amount was agreed.

How do I compare the quotes if the order might change?

A larger credit can look like a saving until the design changes or the launch is canceled. I compare complete scenarios.

I calculate the amount paid if I stop after sampling, place a qualifying order, or buy without meeting the credit conditions. I use the same production scope and delivery terms for both suppliers.

I check the result with simple arithmetic

Suppose Supplier A charges $120 for sampling and $30 for delivery. It offers a $100 credit on a qualifying order. Supplier B charges $60 for sampling and $25 for delivery, with no credit. If I stop after sampling, I have spent $150 with A and $85 with B. If I place a qualifying order, A adds $50 to its production quote after the credit. B adds $85. If my order with A falls below the credit threshold, the full $150 remains in the comparison.

These figures are examples. I still compare the production quotes. A $35 difference in the sample cost after credit can be outweighed by a higher production price, extra freight, or a different order minimum. I use the landed cost guide to compare the full purchase on the same terms. I also check whether the suppliers used the same board, finish and quantity before I treat their prices as comparable.

I add a scenario for a paid revision if I expect one. I confirm whether the second fee is credited or separate. I calculate the resulting total without assuming the final artwork will pass on the first attempt. If the samples differ in what they can establish, I record that beside the figures. A lower fee helps only if the sample answers the question I am paying to resolve.

What should I put in writing before paying?

A quote can describe a generous credit without showing how the production invoice will apply it. I ask for terms that another buyer can verify.

I record the sample deliverables, price, payment timing, eligible credit and revision rules. I ask the supplier to show how those terms appear on the sample and production orders.

I give the buyer a record to check against the invoice

I save the sample quote and the agreed credit wording under the same reference. I ask the supplier to identify the sample fee separately from freight and other costs. Before placing a bulk order, I check the item, specification, quantity and deadline against the eligibility terms. If the purchase qualifies, I ask the supplier to show the credit amount on the production quote before I approve payment.

I also document what happens when I stop. I confirm whether the supplier will still send the finished samples and whether I owe freight or another agreed cost. If I reject a sample because it misses a stated requirement, I check which correction or repeat work the quote covers. A design change I request after approval is a separate event. I make sure the record gives the supplier and my team a shared way to distinguish the two.

When the sample arrives, I check it against the agreed scope. I note what it demonstrates and what still needs review. I make the production decision from that evidence and the complete cost. If I proceed, I reconcile the credit on the invoice and keep the approval with the sample record. If I do not proceed, I close the project with the actual sampling expense.

Conclusion

I compare the full sample cost in each buying scenario and count a credit only when the production order meets the agreed terms.
