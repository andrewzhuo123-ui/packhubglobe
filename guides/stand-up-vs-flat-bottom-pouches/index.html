Stand-up vs flat-bottom pouches: what should you compare?

Use filling, shelf presentation and protection needs to guide format choice.

By PackHubGlobe packaging team · Updated 16 October 2026

Worked exampleComparisonChecklistFAQ

Compare the format before choosing a finish

A stand-up pouch uses a bottom gusset to support the filled pack. A flat-bottom pouch has a defined base and side panels. Their suitability depends on filled-product behavior, target dimensions, closure and packing operations.

Material structure is a separate decision

Pouch shape does not specify oxygen or moisture barrier. State the product, expected shelf life, storage environment, distribution conditions and filling process so a material direction can be reviewed.

Confirm weight and dimensions with a filled sample

The same stated fill weight can occupy different volumes for whole beans, powder or pet treats. Compare physical fit, zipper clearance and seal area using the intended product. A stock bag may help screen shape but does not validate a final printed laminate.

Document the agreed specification

Record size convention, material layers, thickness, printing, zipper or valve, test needs and quantity per design. Any food-contact, recyclability or shelf-life claim needs evidence relevant to the selected structure and intended use.

Quick answer

Stand-up and flat-bottom pouches differ in base construction and presentation. Select the shape around product volume, filling workflow, closure access and shelf arrangement, then specify material and barrier requirements separately. Equal fill weights do not imply equal bag dimensions for products with different bulk density.

A worked procurement example

Illustrative comparison: a coffee buyer wants two pouch styles for the same bean fill. Both samples use the planned closure features and a representative product fill. The team compares front-panel visibility, headspace, standing behavior, zipper access and packing into an outer carton. The trial helps select a size and structure, but it does not establish shelf life or qualify an environmental claim for the final printed material.

Compare the decisions that matter

DecisionWhat to share or review
Filled shapeCompare the actual packed appearance rather than only a flat drawing.
Functional featuresIdentify zipper, valve, window or other features before artwork approval.
MaterialKeep oxygen, moisture, light and seal requirements separate from the pouch silhouette.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing size only from a nominal gram capacity.
  • Selecting a metallic appearance as a substitute for a material specification.
  • Approving artwork before the gussets, seals and usable panels are confirmed.

Use the comparison above to identify what is still unknown. Ask for a written answer or a suitable sample where a purchasing decision depends on that point. An unanswered requirement is easier to resolve before artwork, tooling or a production quantity is approved than after the order has been scheduled.

Your approval checklist

  • Product and target fill quantity
  • Approximate bulk volume or a representative fill
  • Material and shelf-life requirements
  • Closure, valve or window needs
  • Filling and sealing conditions
  • Finished size, panel layout and carton packing

Keep this checklist with the specification and the people responsible for product, artwork and purchasing approval. Give each file and sample a clear revision. If an item remains open, identify who will answer it and which part of the order depends on the answer. This makes the approval useful to the next person reviewing the package, rather than leaving important decisions in an email thread or a photograph without context.

Turn the findings into a clear approval record

Record the reference reviewed, the conditions or contents used, the findings and the changes requested. Distinguish a design direction from an approved production specification. When several people are involved, identify who approves wording, structure, printed appearance and the commercial terms. Keep the final artwork and the relevant physical reference together. If a later revision changes one decision, review the affected parts explicitly rather than silently treating every earlier approval as valid for the new version.

Frequently asked questions

Which format holds more product?

Capacity depends on finished dimensions, gussets, headspace and the product itself. Compare a filled sample of each proposed size.

Does flat-bottom construction provide better barrier?

Shape and barrier are separate decisions. Compare the actual material structures and relevant measured properties for the intended application.

Can one design work on both formats?

The graphic identity can be coordinated, but panels, gussets and seal areas may differ. Use a confirmed dieline for each structure.
