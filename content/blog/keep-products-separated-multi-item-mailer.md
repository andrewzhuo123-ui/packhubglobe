# How do I keep products from touching in a multi-item mailer?

I can separate every item on the packing bench and find them rubbing together after the first trip through a delivery network.

**I give each product a defined position in the multi-item mailer, separate incompatible surfaces, and limit movement above and below the divider. I test the actual order combinations and inspect how the parts shifted before approving the packing method.**

I work from the items that can cause damage and the surfaces that need protection. That helps me decide where a fixed divider is useful and where a separate wrap makes more sense.

## Which products should I separate first?

A divider layout based only on item count can put a heavy bottle next to a soft carton. I start by checking what can hit what.

**I identify hard, heavy and protruding items first. Then I mark the surfaces they could damage and lay out the order so those surfaces stay apart as the parcel moves.**

### I map the contact that would cause a rejection

I place the whole order on a bench. I mark the heavy items, sharp corners and fragile closures. I also mark printed panels, exposed finishes and any small product that could slip under an insert. I check the packed orientation before I decide where to put the divider. A bottle that looks safe upright might lean toward a neighboring carton if its base can slide. A flat accessory might move upward and cross a low wall.

I look at plausible changes in the order mix. If a gift mailer can carry two different bottles, I identify the heavier bottle and the one with the larger closure. I check whether the same separator works with both. I avoid treating an empty cell as harmless because it may leave room for the item beside it to move. I use the [guide to protective inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) when I need to compare ways to hold a product in its cell.

I also decide which marks would count as a failure. A small impression on an internal pad may be acceptable. The same mark on a display panel may make the item unsellable. I give the packing team pictures of the surfaces that matter. That makes it possible to compare the options without asking the team to guess what a mark means.

## When should I use fixed cells or individual wraps?

Loose wrap can suit varied orders, but it takes time to apply. A fixed divider can be faster when the products fit its cells.

**I use fixed cells for repeatable combinations with predictable shapes. I consider individual wraps when orders vary or a surface needs its own protection, then restrain the wrapped items in the outer box.**

### I compare how the options work during a normal shift

I start with a layout the packer can build in the same order every time. A fixed cell tells the packer where to put each item. It can also make a missing item easy to spot. I check that the divider stays upright and that a product does not fit into the wrong cell. When two items look similar, I mark the positions on the packing instruction rather than relying on memory.

| Packing choice | Useful when | What I verify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed cells | The order mix and shapes repeat | Cell fit, wall height and divider position |
| Separate wraps | Individual surfaces need protection or sizes vary | Coverage, restraint and repeatable wrapping |
| Cells plus wrap | A vulnerable surface needs extra protection | Fit with the added thickness and removal effort |

The UPS packaging guidelines discuss protecting items within a shipping package.[^1] I check how the method works with my product, rather than treating wrap as permission to leave an item free to move. I include seams or taped areas when I measure the wrapped item.

I compare the options after packing a few representative orders. I record the extra parts and assembly time. I also ask which parts are most likely to be omitted during a busy shift. A combined design may be worth using when it protects a high-risk surface and remains easy to repeat.

## How do I stop items crossing above or below a divider?

A partition can prevent side contact on the bench and leave a route over the top or through a gap at the base.

**I check the full height of each product and the gap below and above the divider. I close the mailer and confirm that no item can slip into another cell as it moves.**

### I check the divider in the closed package

I put the smallest planned product in its cell and look for a path underneath the divider. I check whether a flexible item can bend through a slot or squeeze through a corner. Then I check the tallest item. I look at where its weight sits and how far its top can lean. I measure the clearance using the actual assembled box. The [guide to inside and outside dimensions](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) helps me separate usable interior space from the dimensions on a catalog listing.

If the divider can lift, I check how it locates at the base and under the closed lid. I may try a tab or a fold that keeps it seated. If the item can pass over the wall, I compare a taller separator with a pad that limits upward motion. I make sure the pad bears on suitable areas. Pressing directly on a pump or cap may introduce a different defect.

I inspect how the final flap and any top pad sit when I close the mailer. If the lid bulges, I check whether one of the parts is being compressed. I also check how the customer removes the contents. A small item that lodges under a folded wall may be hard to see. I revise the layout if I find a hidden pocket or a removal step that brings vulnerable items together again.

## What should I inspect after the packed order is handled?

If I tip out the products before taking pictures, I lose evidence of where they moved. I open the pack in a fixed order.

**I photograph the closed parcel and then the loaded cells before moving anything. I compare item positions and contact marks against the original layout and use the result to revise the packing instructions.**

### I trace fresh marks back to their source

I record the exact contents, insert revision and closure before the trial. I include the lightest and heaviest order combinations that will use the same divider. I check whether both can be packed consistently. FedEx's packing guidance explains how cushioning can fill empty space and help prevent movement.[^2] I test the complete pack with a handling plan suited to the shipment. I keep quick bench checks separate from the results of any formal distribution test.

When I open a sample, I look for tilted products, compressed edges and pads that have moved. I photograph each cell and then lift each item in the same order. If I find matching marks on two items, I compare their heights and directions. If a pad has torn, I identify what rubbed against it. I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to make the record easy to repeat across teams.

I update the packing instructions after the revised sample meets the acceptance criteria. I show the component order, item orientation and where each wrap or pad goes. I also write down which order combinations need a different carton. This keeps the method usable when the warehouse adds a new bundle or replaces an item with a larger version.

## Conclusion

I protect products by separating the surfaces that can collide, controlling movement in the closed mailer, and checking the result before standardizing the packing method.

[^2]: [FedEx packing guidance](https://www.fedex.com/en-ca/resources-tools/pack.html) discusses using cushioning to fill empty spaces.
