Sourcing & cost

How do I specify an acceptable quantity overrun?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can agree to a small percentage of extra packaging and still receive more of a seasonal design than I can use.

I write the maximum accepted quantity for each order line and specify how extra pieces will be priced. I agree on when the supplier must ask before shipping and give receiving the same limits.

I start with the pieces I can use. Then I compare that range with the supplier's production plan and put the result on the order.

Should the tolerance apply by design or to the whole order?

An order total can land within tolerance while one design arrives short and another takes up more space than planned.

I set limits by item, size or artwork version when the pieces cannot substitute for one another. I add a total limit if I also need to control overall spend or storage.

I show the supplier which quantities cannot be combined

I give the supplier a target for each artwork file and list the acceptable range in pieces. I distinguish a production minimum from my minimum usable receipt. A supplier may have to run enough sheets to meet several targets. I still need to know how many of each design will be available after printing and converting. I use the custom packaging MOQ guide to clarify where a supplier's minimum applies.

Illustrative item Target Minimum usable Maximum accepted
Standard carton 2,000 2,000 2,100
Seasonal carton 400 400 410
Shared insert 2,400 2,400 2,500

I choose these ranges for the example. On a real order I ask how long the extra pieces will stay useful and whether they can serve another product. I may accept more extra plain inserts than seasonal cartons. If the buyer sets a total maximum for all cartons, I check that it works alongside the individual limits and explain which cap takes precedence.

Esko describes production planning that considers quantities and overruns when comparing sheet layouts.1 I ask the converter which mix it can make and how it will handle extra output. I record the agreed finished quantities. If the supplier cannot meet them at the proposed price, I compare a different production layout or a different quantity instead of leaving the range open.

How do I choose the maximum extra quantity I can use?

A default percentage does not tell me what the extra pieces are worth. I check how long I can use them and where they will go.

I base the cap on realistic future use, storage capacity and the cost of excess pieces. I use whole units and confirm rounding when the supplier quotes a percentage.

I compare the overrun with the next planned order

For a current design used every month, extra cartons may reduce my next purchase. I check how soon the business expects to need them and whether an artwork change is planned. For a dated campaign, I set a lower cap if the extra units could become obsolete. I also check how the pieces are packed. An extra partial case may need a separate storage location or a different count at receipt.

I work through the cost using the proposed price for those units. If 100 extra pieces cost $0.48 each, the additional invoice would be $48 before any related handling or freight changes. I check whether I can use all 100 and whether that extra quantity changes the shipment. A small overrun can add another case even when it fits inside the budget. I compare that effect with the benefit of having replacement stock.

I confirm the actual number when the supplier uses a percentage. For 175 units, a 3% calculation gives 5.25 pieces. I record whether the maximum is five or six extra pieces and write the resulting cap on the order. I avoid leaving the warehouse to choose a rounding rule. If the supplier wants a wider range because of its production process, I ask for the cost of tightening it so I can make the tradeoff explicitly.

When can a supplier ship and charge for extra units?

An allowed production range can be confused with permission to bill every extra piece. I spell out the decision before the order runs.

I state whether the supplier may ship extra units automatically, how they are priced, and when it must get approval. I ask for quantities by line before dispatch.

I separate the quantity limit from the invoice terms

I identify the units the buyer authorizes in advance. For example, I may accept and pay for up to 100 extra standard cartons at the stated unit price. I may require separate written approval for any extra seasonal cartons. I record whether freight and case packing are included in the quoted unit price. I use the packaging quote checklist to keep those commercial terms visible alongside the quantity range.

I also agree on what happens above the cap. I ask the supplier to report the quantity available before packing the shipment. If the output exceeds the agreed amount, I ask it to separate those pieces while we decide what to do. I record who can authorize extra stock and how that decision appears on the order. An informal note from someone who cannot approve spend creates a problem for receiving and accounts payable.

I keep overruns separate from rejected pieces. If the supplier ships a target plus extras, I ask it to report the usable units and identify any nonconforming stock that was separately authorized. I confirm the minimum acceptable count after inspection. That keeps the overrun from hiding a shortage of acceptable pieces.

How should receiving handle a delivery outside the range?

A warehouse can receive the right number of cases and still count the wrong number of one artwork version. I give it the order line limits.

I compare counted units and condition against each order line. I hold exceptions for the buyer's decision and reconcile the final approved quantity with the invoice.

I make the packing list and order agree

I ask for an item code, artwork revision where relevant, case count and piece count. I identify partial cases. At receiving I compare the labels with the packing list and record any difference between the shipped, counted and accepted quantities. If one variant is above its cap and another is below its floor, I log both exceptions. I do not offset the two when they serve different products.

Oracle's receiving documentation describes controls that can allow, warn about or reject receipts above a configured tolerance.2 I choose the receiving instruction that fits the order. I check whether my system can represent the exact limits we agreed. If it only holds a percentage, I include the whole-unit cap in the order notes and make sure the warehouse knows which decision to escalate.

I use the landed cost comparison guide when I reconcile the accepted count with the final amount paid. I record any authorized freight change or extra units. I save the actual delivery quantity and any issue with the order. On the next buy I can check whether the agreed range was practical or whether it needs to change. I also track whether the warehouse used the extra pieces before the artwork expired. That result helps me decide how much flexibility to offer on the next run.

Conclusion

I set a usable quantity range for each line, agree on payment for extra pieces, and give the warehouse a clear rule for exceptions.
