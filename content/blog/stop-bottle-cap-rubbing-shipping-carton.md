# How do I stop a bottle cap rubbing inside a shipping carton?

I can protect a bottle from breaking and still receive complaints about scratched caps. The marks can start before the parcel leaves the packing bench.

**I find the surface that touches the cap, then support the bottle body or shoulder to keep the cap clear. I check the actual closed parcel and repeat the handling test with the revised insert.**

I separate visible scuffs from closure damage and leakage. I use the mark and its position to decide what to inspect first.

## How do I identify the surface that touches the cap?

A scratched cap tells me where the damage appeared. It does not identify which part of the pack caused it.

**I photograph the cap before packing, after closing and after handling. I compare the location of each mark with the nearest divider, lid, bottle or protective sleeve.**

### I record the direction and position of the mark

I put the bottle in its normal packing position and take a picture of the top and side of the cap. I note how the bottle is oriented in the cell. If the carton holds several bottles, I give each cell a location such as front left or back right. A row of marks on the same side of several caps gives me a place to inspect first. I compare their height with the top edge of the divider. A mark confined to the top of the cap sends me to the underside of the lid or the flap overlap.

I open a second sample immediately after packing it. This check can show whether the bottle rubs as it passes through the insert or whether a flap drags across the cap during closure. A mark that is already present after this step calls for a different fix from one that appears only after motion. I document both stages so the team can repeat the observation. I use the [damage inspection record](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) when I need a warehouse to photograph the pack in the same order.

I record leaks or a closure that has shifted separately. If the closure no longer looks or feels correctly seated, I hold the sample and ask the product team how to check it. Removing a visible rub mark does not resolve a closure defect.

## How do I support the bottle without loading the cap?

Adding a pad above a loose bottle can put more pressure on the cap. I first look for a stable part of the bottle to support.

**I locate the bottle at the base and a suitable body or shoulder area. I keep the closure clear of the insert, divider edge and lid throughout packing and handling.**

### I check movement in both directions

I start at the base. A pocket can prevent the bottle from sliding sideways, but it may still allow the top to swing. I check where the shoulder moves when I gently tilt the packed sample. If the neck passes through an upper support, I check the gap around the cap during insertion as well as after the bottle is seated. I verify that the support contacts a part of the bottle that can carry the load. If the label is delicate, I check whether that new point of contact leaves a mark of its own.

| What I see | What I check next |
| --- | --- |
| Scuff at divider height | Clearance as the bottle leans toward the divider |
| Circular mark on the cap top | Closed lid position and movement toward the lid |
| Marks after hand packing alone | The cap's path past the insert and flaps |
| Marks between two caps | Movement in each cell and the height of the separator |

I use the [guide to fragile product inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) to compare support choices. For a tall bottle with a narrow body, I might try a deeper bottom cell and a shaped shoulder support. For a short bottle in a tight cell, I might need to change only the divider edge. I change one feature at a time when I can. This makes it easier to tell whether the improvement came from less movement or simply from moving the contact point.

## What do I check after the shipping carton is closed?

A visible gap in an open carton can disappear after the flaps overlap. I measure the packed condition that will leave the warehouse.

**I close the carton with the usual flaps, tape and contents. I check the closest points above and beside the cap, including near seams, then repeat the check with representative bottle and carton samples.**

### I account for the tallest packed position

I identify the bottle and closure combination that sits highest in the support. I check the bottle after filling and closing when those steps affect its shape or position. I include every insert, bag and divider that will go into the order. I check the lid at the point closest to the cap and note whether that point is under an overlapping flap. The [box dimensions guide](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) helps me distinguish the available inside space from the outside height on a carton specification.

For liquids, I keep the chosen leak containment in the trial. FedEx's customer packing guide includes liquid packing illustrations that show a bottle in a sealed bag.[^1] I check the carrier's guidance for the actual shipment. A bag seam or gathered material can change where a bottle sits, so a trial without the intended secondary packing can give a different result. I keep tape and pack closure consistent when comparing samples.

I inspect both a recently assembled carton and a representative sample from production stock. If the lid only closes when I force it down, I treat that as a reason to inspect the geometry. I ask the supplier to check the complete stack of parts. Extra clearance on the drawing may disappear once I include folded board, a raised base, a separator and the real range of bottle heights.

## How do I confirm that the fix survives the delivery route?

A revised insert can look good on the bench and still let the bottle move in a parcel network. I compare complete packed samples.

**I document the original and revised packs, then check both under a handling plan suited to the route. I inspect cap marks, closure condition, bottle movement and any new damage at the support points.**

### I set acceptance criteria before testing

I specify which cap surfaces must remain free of visible scuffs and which light conditions I will use for inspection. I record a reference photograph so another person can compare the same area. If the product team also requires a seal or closure check, I record that result separately. I repeat the normal packing steps and retain the exact version of the insert for each sample. That lets me tell whether a new result came from the design or a different assembly method.

I ask a test provider to recommend a method based on the actual shipping route and load. ISTA lists a general simulation procedure for eligible individual parcels and separate procedures for other distribution conditions.[^2] I distinguish a bench trial used to compare options from a documented distribution test. If the warehouse sometimes ships one bottle and sometimes several, I bring both configurations. They place different loads on the divider and can show different patterns of movement.

After the trial, I inspect the same points in the same order. I look for fresh marks on the cap, dents on the lid, wear on the divider and scratches at any new support point. I photograph the bottle in place before lifting it. If the revised design meets the criteria, I update the packing instruction with the correct insert, orientation and closure method. I retain the sample reference so the warehouse can recognize an approved pack.

## Conclusion

I identify the contact, support the bottle away from the cap, and check the revised carton with the normal pack-out and an appropriate handling test.

[^1]: [FedEx customer packaging guidelines](https://www.fedex.com/content/dam/fedex/eu-europe/downloads/PL_EN_2024_8_web_pdf_NA_Packing_Instructions_marketing.pdf) includes liquid packing examples.
[^2]: [ISTA test procedures](https://ista.org/test_procedures.php?test=3A) describes general simulation tests and their distribution settings.
