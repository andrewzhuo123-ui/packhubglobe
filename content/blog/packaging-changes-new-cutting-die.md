# Which packaging changes need a new cutting die?

I can request a small change to a carton and trigger a new tooling quote. The effect depends on what has to move on the tool.

**I ask for a cutting die review when a cut, crease, perforation, or other feature made by the tool changes. I ask the converter to confirm reuse, modification, or replacement. For graphics alone, I check the print setup separately.**

I work from the last approved structure and identify the exact change. That gives the converter something concrete to inspect before I approve a tooling charge.

## What does the cutting die actually control?

I might call a revised PDF a new dieline even when only the logo moved. That wording makes a simple quote harder to compare.

**I separate the graphic artwork from the lines that control cutting and folding. I compare those structural lines at full scale before asking whether the physical tool can still produce the carton.**

### I start with the original manufacturing drawing

I ask for the version of the structural drawing tied to the last accepted order. I compare the edge of the blank, window openings, crease lines, slots and any tear features against the proposed revision. Esko describes a structural design file as a record of cut and crease lines that is distinct from the graphics.[^1] That distinction helps when a PDF contains both kinds of information. It lets me tell the supplier exactly which file has changed and what I believe the change affects.

For example, I might move a printed frame away from a fold. The frame moves on the artwork, while the fold stays in the same place on the structural drawing. I ask for an updated print proof and check that the carton still uses the existing structural revision. If I move the fold itself so the front panel becomes wider, I send the revised structure for a tooling review.

I use the [packaging dieline guide](/guides/prepare-packaging-dielines/) to assemble the files. I include a marked view as well as the clean drawing, because a subtle difference can disappear in a crowded plan. I check that the files have the same scale and orientation before I compare them. I also ask the converter to confirm the physical die reference. A drawing in my folder shows the intended geometry. The converter's tooling record shows which tool it can actually put on press.

## Which changes make me ask about a replacement?

A change can leave the box's outer dimensions intact and still affect the way it locks. I check the working features as well as the size.

**I request a tooling review for a resized panel, moved fold, new opening, or changed tab, slot, or perforation. I ask whether the existing tool can be altered and what the revised sample needs to prove.**

### I follow the feature through assembly

If a retail carton needs a wider thumb notch, I mark the cut that changes and identify where the customer's finger meets the opening. If a glue flap gets longer, I ask which edge must move and whether the folding and gluing equipment needs a new setup. If a tuck tab starts catching on the product, I compare the tab and the receiving slot together. A tab can fit on a flat drawing and still be hard to close after the carton is filled.

| Proposed change | What I ask the converter to check |
| --- | --- |
| Deeper side panel | Positions of adjacent creases and cuts |
| Larger display window | Cut shape, surrounding board, and window film placement |
| Different tear line | Perforation rule, opening action, and effect on handling |
| Longer glue flap | Trim line, overlap, and folder-gluer setup |

I ask whether a modification can be made consistently across the tool. A production die may make several blanks in one press stroke. If the tool has multiple positions, I ask whether every position will receive the change and how they will be checked. The supplier also needs to consider the supporting operations. A new window can affect removal of waste pieces even if the change looks modest on the blank.

I request separate quotes for feasible modification and replacement. I want the lead time, sample allowance, expected output, and whether modifying the tool would prevent a repeat order of the older carton.

## Can I reuse the tool if the board or finish changes?

A carton can keep the same drawing and behave differently on a thicker stock. I check the stock and the tooling together.

**I ask the converter to test the new board against the existing die and crease setup. For a finish change, I ask which separate tools or processes need updating.**

### I check the fold and the tool that makes it

I give the converter the current and proposed stock details and ask for a recommendation based on the actual materials. If I only have a board trade name, I ask for the relevant thickness and construction information. I compare the way the new stock folds, closes, and fits around the product. If the pack runs through automatic equipment, I also ask the line team to review the new sample under its normal conditions.

Esko's documentation explains how counters support creasing by giving the stock room to flex under the creasing rule.[^2] I ask whether the change affects the counter or crease setup even if the cutting geometry stays in place. I look for cracked surfaces, a flap that springs back, or a closure that needs more force than the current pack. I keep the old sample nearby for a direct comparison.

For a new surface effect, I ask the converter which operation makes it. A raised mark can involve embossing tooling. A foil design can have its own tooling depending on the process. The existing cutting die might still be usable if the structure and stock work with it. I use the [guide to foil, spot UV and embossing](/guides/foil-vs-spot-uv-vs-embossing/) to name the effect consistently, then ask for the supplier's process and any extra charges as separate lines.

## What do I require before approving the tooling quote?

A quote can say “new die” without naming the work it covers. I want the invoice and the eventual sample to match the change I requested.

**I ask for the affected tooling, drawing revision, lead time, and acceptance sample in writing. I approve the chosen route after the converter identifies what stays in service and what changes.**

### I make approval refer to an observable result

For a carton that needs a bigger opening, I might ask to see the formed carton around the actual product. I include the agreed opening dimensions and the edge positions that matter. I ask for an example of the closing action so I can see whether the flap still catches. When a hand-cut sample can show the proposed geometry, I use it to review fit. I then agree with the converter how to check the result made on the production setup, since a hand sample cannot demonstrate every machine result.

I ask the converter to list each affected item, such as the main cutting die, the crease counter, or separate finishing tools. I ask whether the quoted charge includes trial material and a retained sample. The [packaging quote checklist](/guides/packaging-quote-checklist/) helps me keep those amounts visible when comparing suppliers. I also record the effect on repeat orders if the old tool will be changed or retired.

I keep the marked drawing, clean revision, quote and approved physical sample reference together. If a supplier proposes an adjustment during setup, I ask for the updated drawing and a fresh check of the affected feature. A buyer placing the next order should be able to tell which version was accepted and which physical tool the converter intends to use.

## Conclusion

I compare the structural revisions, confirm the affected tooling with the converter, and approve the change against a sample that shows how the carton will work.

[^1]: [Esko structural design files](https://docs.esko.com/docs/en-us/icutlayout/18/userguide/en-us/common/st/concept/co_aboutardfiles.html) describes the distinction between structural lines and artwork.
[^2]: [Esko documentation on creasing counters](https://docs.esko.com/docs/en-us/artioscad/23.07/userguide/en-us/common/ac/topic/UG3_Designer_id1055268UER23.html) describes their role in folding carton tooling.
