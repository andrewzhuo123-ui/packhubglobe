Shipping & protection

How can I reduce scuff marks on printed retail cartons in transit?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can receive intact products and still reject the cartons because a row of dark front panels arrived with pale rub marks.

I locate the surface that rubs each carton and determine when the mark appears. I compare a change in packing or separation with a change in print finish, then test the complete shipping configuration.

I keep the affected boxes in the position where I found them. The pattern of marks helps me decide which comparison to make first.

How do I identify what rubbed the carton?

A close-up shows the damage, but I need the position of the box and its neighbors to explain the pattern.

I photograph the cartons before unloading, mark their positions, and compare each scuff with nearby carton edges, pads and case walls. I record which faces were touching.

I look for matching marks on both surfaces

I take a picture of the full case from above and label the rows in the inspection record. I photograph the visible display panels before lifting anything. If a pale line appears at the same height across several boxes, I compare it with the edge of the separator or the neighboring carton. I inspect both surfaces for a corresponding mark. If there is colored material on the other surface, I record its location and appearance. That gives the printer or converter something to inspect without assuming how the transfer happened.

I check a carton taken from the same production batch before packing. I also open a newly packed case before it has traveled. If a mark is already visible, I look at forming, insertion and case loading. If the marks appear only on shipped cases, I compare their positions and packing conditions. I use the warehouse damage inspection guide to keep the photographs and timing consistent.

I distinguish a rub from a crease, dent or scratch. A sharp edge can cut a line into the finish. A broad contact area may dull a glossy panel. I describe what I see and save a clean sample for comparison. I keep the affected carton and nearby packing material together while the team investigates.

What can I change in the way the cartons are packed?

A separator can protect a printed face and still let the boxes slide together if it folds or moves out of position.

I reduce relative movement and separate the surfaces that rub. I compare a different orientation, a smooth separator or a fitted support using the same outer case.

I try the smallest change that addresses the contact

Mark location Packing change I compare
Front face against another carton Rotate the load or place a smooth separator between faces
Corner beside the case wall Adjust the support or check the available clearance
Line beside a divider edge Move or soften the contact edge and check divider position
Marks on the top row Check the top pad and movement under the case flaps

I keep the carton count and product load the same for the first comparison. If I change the orientation, I check whether the new arrangement brings a vulnerable surface against the case wall. I also look at the way cartons are inserted and removed. A narrow opening can cause a rub during loading even when the final position is safe. I show the intended sequence to the packer and watch for contact in both directions.

I use the guide to protective inserts when I need a part to hold the cartons in place. I check that the support stays seated after closing and does not press on the decorated area. I also compare the time and material needed for each option. A separator is easier to standardize when the team knows which face it protects and can verify its position before closing the case.

When should I ask the printer to review the finish?

I can change the packing layout and still see damage when the print faces touch during normal handling. I check the printed surface itself.

I ask the printer to review the ink, board and finish when marks appear before shipping or under unavoidable contact. I compare representative samples under an agreed abrasion check.

I test the surface separately from the shipping pack

I send the printer a clean sample, an affected sample and the material that touched it when available. I identify the finish and artwork revision. I ask whether the sample represents the normal print and finishing process. I check if the issue appears on one ink color, on a heavily printed area or across the whole panel. I use the packaging print proof guide to keep the comparison tied to the specified stock and finish.

ASTM publishes a Sutherland rub method for assessing abrasion resistance of printed materials.1 I ask the printer or lab whether an applicable method can compare the actual constructions. I agree on the surface to assess and how to judge the result. A flat specimen comparison can show how two finishes respond under the selected conditions. I still use an assembled trial to check the contact that occurs inside the shipment.

If a new finish performs better, I check whether it changes color, gloss, folding or any statement about the materials. I request a representative finished carton rather than accepting a swatch alone. I compare the cost with a packing adjustment that could solve the same problem. I record the accepted option with the print specification.

How do I verify the correction before the next shipment?

A clean sample from one bench check does not tell me how the full shipping case behaves through its route.

I compare the original and revised packs under representative conditions. I inspect the same faces before and after handling, then approve the exact packing and print configuration that passed.

I agree on the appearance check before testing

I identify the faces the customer sees and the marks that would cause rejection. I agree on the light and viewing distance for inspection. I compare the new sample with an accepted reference. If the route needs a formal transit test, I ask the provider to recommend a procedure for the complete packed product. ISTA describes general simulation tests for distribution conditions such as vibration and impact.2 I keep that result separate from the print abrasion comparison.

I pack the original and revised configurations using the intended process. I record the case, number of cartons, orientation, separators and closure. I inspect each sample without changing the arrangement first. I check both the display panels and the surfaces facing a divider or case wall. If the revised pack removes the first mark but introduces another, I record the new contact and adjust the design.

When the revised version passes, I update the packing instruction and identify the accepted finish and case layout. I retain photographs showing where the separators sit. I ask receiving or the fulfilment team to check the same faces on the next delivery. Their observations help me verify that the correction survived ordinary production and packing.

Conclusion

I trace each scuff to its contact point, compare packing and finish changes, and verify the chosen correction on the complete shipment.
