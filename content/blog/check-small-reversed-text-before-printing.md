# How do I check small reversed text before printing?

I can read small white text on a bright screen and find that its letters close up on the printed carton.

**I inspect the smallest letters at final size, check how the text and background separate into inks, and read a representative printed sample. I approve the copy only when the required words remain clear on the finished pack.**

I check the artwork and the printed result separately. The first shows how the file is built. The second shows what a reader will see.

## What should I measure in the smallest line?

The point size tells me how the type is set. It can hide a thin stroke or a narrow opening inside a letter.

**I check the thinnest strokes and smallest open spaces at final size. I ask the printer for limits that match the press, stock, background ink and finish.**

### I identify the letters that are most likely to lose detail

I print or view the artwork at the actual dimensions. I check the smallest line and note which characters are hard to distinguish. I look closely at narrow openings in letters such as e and a, the gap between adjacent characters, and the dots or accents that carry meaning. I also check small punctuation. Losing a decimal point or a separator can change what a reader understands even if the rest of the line looks clear.

I send the exact line to the printer and identify the stock and print process from the quote. I ask for the minimum feature size it recommends for reversed type in this construction. I ask whether the guidance assumes one background ink or several. The Esko packaging preflight documentation describes checks that can help teams find print issues in packaging artwork.[^1] I use the printer's own criteria when deciding whether a flagged line can stay as it is.

I try a sturdier typeface or a slightly larger line where the openings are small. I keep a copy of the original for comparison. If the revised line no longer fits, I check whether other copy can move to a different panel. I keep the meaning and required information intact. I want a proof the reader can use without needing to enlarge it on a screen.

## How do I check that the light text will print as intended?

A white object in the layout may be intended to leave paper exposed or to print with white ink. I confirm which one the file represents.

**I check the intended ink layers, knockout and overprint settings with the printer. I inspect the exported production file so I can see which parts of the background remain under the letters.**

### I compare the composite view with the separations

I identify what makes the letters light. On white paperboard, the design may leave a gap in the dark background so the board shows through. On a dark or transparent material, the design may require a separate white ink. I ask the printer to confirm that choice before I edit the file. A mark that looks white in a composite preview does not tell me how the press will produce it.

| What I see in the artwork | What I ask the printer to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Light type on white board | Which background inks should be absent in the letters |
| White type on clear or colored material | Whether a white ink separation is required |
| Small type over a multi-ink dark area | Whether ink alignment could create a fringe |
| Type crossing an effect or coating | How the finish will affect the reading area |

Adobe explains how to preview individual color separations in InDesign.[^2] I ask the printer to review the exported production file using its own prepress workflow. I use the [print proof guide](/guides/packaging-print-proof/) to tie that review to the exact revision. I also check that the letters stay clear of creases and seams after forming the pack. I send both the layout view and the details the printer needs to reproduce the intended result.

## What should I look for on the printed sample?

An enlarged photo can reveal a print defect. It cannot tell me by itself whether a person can read the text at normal size.

**I read the finished sample at normal distance and then inspect the smallest letters closely. I look for filled openings, broken details, color fringes and glare across the line.**

### I use two viewing distances for different questions

I start at the distance I expect for ordinary reading. I ask a colleague to read the line on the actual pack and tell me where they hesitated. I check the sample upright and at an angle. On a glossy surface, a light can reflect across one end of the line. I record that separately from poor definition in the printed letters. I also check whether a fold bends the line away from view.

Then I inspect the difficult characters with magnification. I compare the same words across several samples where they are available. A fringe along one edge suggests a different question from a consistently filled letter opening. I give the printer the sample reference and a close photograph with the affected line marked. I ask whether the issue comes from the file, ink alignment, stock or print process before choosing a correction.

I use the [guide to packaging sample types](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to confirm what the proof can demonstrate. A screen proof can establish wording and placement. A representative printed sample can reveal effects of the ink, stock and finish. If the trial uses a different process, I ask how the production result will be checked before the rest of the order proceeds.

## What do I change if the text remains hard to read?

A printer can make a precise line that still takes too much effort to read. I change the feature that caused the problem and check it again.

**I enlarge or simplify the type, increase the clear spacing, or move the copy to a calmer background. I approve the revised sample against the same reading and print checks.**

### I keep the correction tied to the defect

If the inside of a letter fills, I compare a larger size or a typeface with more open letter shapes. If the line has a colored fringe, I ask the printer whether a simpler background would improve the result. If glare obscures the text, I compare a different position or surface finish. I use the [guide to brand color across materials](/guides/brand-color-across-materials/) when a change to the background affects a color that the brand needs to keep consistent.

I review the revised line at the actual panel size. I confirm that the change leaves enough space around other required information and does not move a word onto a fold. I check the entire group of sizes if several packs share the design. The smallest panel may have a different line break or place the text closer to the closure.

I keep the approved print sample and the artwork revision together. I record the stock, finish and any prepress decision that mattered. When a supplier changes one of those inputs, I ask for another check of the same line. At reorder I can compare the sample in hand with an accepted result and see whether the change affects legibility.

## Conclusion

I check the file, read the finished sample at normal size, and retain the approved artwork and print settings for the next order.
