Sourcing & cost

What should I ask before accepting a cheaper board substitution?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can approve a lower board price and find that the new cartons take longer to close or arrive with visible crease cracks.

I ask for the exact replacement grade and compare it with the approved board. I test both in the same carton and packing process, then check whether the savings remain after changes in yield, labor and performance.

I review a proposed board substitution as a change to a finished package. I keep the current carton as a control so I can see which differences matter in use.

Which material details should I compare first?

Two boards can have the same weight per square metre and behave differently in a folded carton. I ask what else changed.

I request both grade sheets and compare thickness, stiffness, surface, grain direction and relevant tolerances. I ask the supplier to identify the proposed mill or source and state what is guaranteed.

I compare the exact grades on the quote

I start with the grade listed on the current purchase specification. I ask the supplier to identify the replacement by its full code. If the replacement is described by a category alone, I ask which actual grade will run. I keep the data sheet and supplier confirmation with the quote. I check whether either document gives a typical result or an agreed limit. A typical number can help me compare samples, but I need to know what the supplier commits to delivering.

Property Why I ask about it
Grammage and thickness They describe different aspects of the sheet
Stiffness and grain direction They help frame a comparison of folding and handling
Surface and coating They can affect print appearance and bonding
Grade and source They let me check that the approved sample matches the order

Holmen's paperboard technical information discusses several properties used to describe board performance.1 I use it to ask for comparable values and methods. If the documents use different test directions or conditions, I ask the supplier to explain the difference before comparing the numbers. ASTM also publishes a method for paper and paperboard thickness.2 I ask what procedure the supplier used and whether the quoted tolerance applies to the delivered material. I use the packaging materials guide to keep the terminology clear when sharing the comparison internally.

What should I test on the formed carton?

A flat swatch can look suitable and still crack at a fold, show a color shift or close differently around the product.

I request a carton made from each board using comparable artwork, tooling and finish. I inspect the folds, closures and print, then load the actual product and check the fit.

I look at the parts that change when paper is folded

I ask the converter to use the approved drawing and to identify any change needed to crease, cut or glue the new grade. I keep that change in the record. If the converter changes the crease setup to make the replacement work, I compare the finished carton made with that setup. I also ask whether the supplier can reproduce it on the production line. The packaging sample types guide helps me decide when I need a production-representative sample.

I check the folded corners and the surfaces that customers see. I look for visible cracking, rough edges and differences in print contrast. I inspect any coated area that passes over a crease. I open and close the carton with the actual product inside. If a tuck flap springs out or the box no longer sits square, I record the location and compare it with the control carton. I check that a print change is acceptable even when the structure works.

I also inspect the carton in its normal outer case or mailer. I check the fit of the complete packed unit. A small increase in thickness can change how several cartons fit beside one another. If the original pack has a documented transport requirement, I ask which checks need to be repeated before accepting the change.

How do I find out whether the packing line will lose time?

A sample that closes by hand may behave differently in a carton feeder. I check the operation that creates the most risk.

I run representative cartons through the actual packing method and record jams, missed closures, rework and handling time. I compare usable output with the current board under similar conditions.

I separate material behavior from setup changes

I agree with the packing team on the same product, insert and order size for both trials. I ask someone to note changes in machine settings or hand assembly sequence. A replacement board may need a different setting and still perform acceptably. I record that setting and ask whether the team can use it consistently. If the new setup adds time at each changeover, I include that time in the comparison.

I count cartons that need to be straightened, opened again or discarded. I record the point where each problem appears. For example, a carton might feed correctly and fail only after the product presses against the closure. If the fault follows a particular batch of blanks, I keep samples from that batch. I give the converter the actual carton and the line observations so it can investigate the same condition.

I also ask how the team will identify the new material at receipt. Two board grades can look similar once printed. I ask for a lot or grade reference on the shipping paperwork and identify when the new board may enter normal production. This gives me a way to compare later deliveries with the accepted trial.

What do I write down before approving the cheaper grade?

A price reduction can disappear once I include higher rejects, extra setup or a change to the box specification.

I compare the cost of usable cartons after setup, packing labor and rejects. I approve the exact grade and any process changes in writing, with the evidence and limits that support the decision.

I calculate the savings after the trial

I use the packaging quote checklist to compare the same order quantity and delivery terms. I include any extra tooling, inspection or adjustment needed for the new board. I account for the time the team observed and the accepted output. If the trial is too small to estimate a reliable reject rate, I keep that uncertainty visible and agree on what to check at the next run. I avoid turning a short demonstration into a prediction about every future order.

I check whether the replacement affects a statement on the pack or a customer requirement. If the grade or construction supports a particular claim, I review that evidence for the new board before releasing the artwork. I record any markets or products that use a different requirement. The proposed material may work for one carton and need a separate review for another.

I save the accepted sample, grade, supplier and revised production settings with the order. I ask for notice before another substitution. If the trial misses a key condition, I keep the existing material in use while the supplier proposes a correction. A later reviewer can see which version was accepted and whether the quoted savings depended on a specific setup.

Conclusion

I accept a cheaper board when the exact grade passes the carton and packing checks, and the savings remain after I compare usable output.
