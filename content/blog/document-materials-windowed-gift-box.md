# How do I document the materials in a windowed gift box?

I can order a paper gift box and discover that its window, liner and adhesive were all left out of the material record.

**I document the finished box by component and identify the materials and attachments for each one. I ask the supplier to match the record to the approved sample, drawing and order, and I check any proposed substitution before release.**

I build the record from a real box. That lets me identify which materials are visible, which are bonded together and which still need confirmation from the converter.

## What components should I include?

A windowed gift box can look like one item on a purchase order. It may contain several parts that change independently.

**I list the structural board, wrap or coating, window, adhesive and insert. I include ribbons, magnets and other attachments when present, and identify which pieces can be removed.**

### I open the box and map each part

I photograph the assembled box and then lay out the parts that can be removed without damage. I identify the lid, base, insert and any sleeve or ribbon. I look inside the lid to see how the window is fitted. I ask the converter to identify anything hidden by a lining or wrap. If the window is held between two board layers, I record those layers instead of giving the whole lid one generic material name.

| Part | Detail I ask the supplier to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Box body | Board grade, wrap and surface treatment |
| Window | Material reference, thickness and outline |
| Window attachment | Adhesive or other attachment and its location |
| Insert | Material, finish, fit and whether it can be removed |

I record which parts belong to one finished box and which are supplied separately. Oracle's bill of material guidance describes using component and quantity records for an assembly.[^1] I use that approach to tie the parts to the box reference. The [packaging materials guide](/guides/packaging-materials-explained/) helps me give the same names to components when I talk with the supplier and designer. I save a marked photo so another buyer can match the list to the actual box.

## What should I ask about the window and its attachment?

A clear patch can look the same after a material change. I ask for identifiers that can be checked when the next order arrives.

**I request the window material, grade or supplier code, thickness, and attachment method. I record where it is bonded and what happens when someone tries to remove it.**

### I distinguish the film from the way it is held

I ask whether the clear part is a single film, a laminate, or another construction. I ask for its exact designation and the specification the converter uses. I record the outline of the film and the size of the opening separately. The patch can extend beyond the cutout to create space for bonding. I use the [dieline preparation guide](/guides/prepare-packaging-dielines/) to request a drawing that shows both the cut and the attachment area clearly.

I ask whether the supplier uses dots, a continuous line, or a separate frame to hold the window. I record the adhesive reference where the supplier can share it and ask how to identify an approved equivalent if it cannot. I note whether the part peels away, tears the board, or stays fixed during ordinary handling. I make that observation on the finished box. A material sample on its own cannot show what the glued assembly does.

The 4evergreen design guideline discusses windows and attachments in fibre-based packaging.[^2] I use it to frame the questions about the completed construction. If I need evidence for a disposal instruction, I review the relevant market and actual box separately. I keep an observation about removal distinct from a conclusion about how the pack will be collected or processed.

## How do I handle missing or confidential material details?

A converter may know the exact adhesive but share only a product family name. I record the gap and decide which evidence the purchase needs.

**I ask for enough information to identify the approved construction and detect substitutions. I record which details are confirmed, which are pending, and who must resolve any gap before release.**

### I agree on a reference the supplier can maintain

I list the evidence available for each component. A technical sheet can identify a grade or intended construction. A supplier declaration may connect that grade to the finished box. A photograph can show where the part appears without establishing its composition. I write down what each piece of evidence supports. If a detail is important for an acceptance or customer requirement, I ask the responsible team what documentation it needs. I do not fill the gap with a guess based on appearance.

If the converter cannot share a commercial code, I ask whether it can provide a controlled internal identifier and written confirmation that it will notify me before changing the component. I ask how it will associate that identifier with the order and delivered lot. I use the [packaging quote checklist](/guides/packaging-quote-checklist/) to keep requests for documentation visible alongside the cost and sample requirements.

I record the pending questions and the person responsible for answering them. I also distinguish material unknowns that affect an approval from details that can be kept in a supplier-controlled specification. That helps me decide whether to hold the order or proceed within a clearly defined scope.

## How do I keep the record useful on later orders?

An approved box can change without looking different from the front. I connect the material record to the order and check it when the supplier proposes a replacement.

**I identify the accepted sample, drawing, material revision and applicable order or lot. I require notice before changes and reassess the affected parts, instructions and claims before approving them.**

### I compare each proposed change with the last accepted version

I put the material record beside the order and sample photographs. I ask the supplier to confirm the box revision and identify any component substitutions before production. If the window changes thickness, I check both fit and the way it attaches. If the adhesive changes, I ask whether it affects the bond or the removal behavior I previously assessed. I keep the old and new details visible in the record so the warehouse can identify which packs are affected.

I review the delivered samples against the accepted construction. If the case label does not identify the version, I ask the supplier to connect the shipment paperwork with its production record. I record which lot I checked and what I observed. A complete record makes it possible to investigate a complaint about one delivery without assuming it applies to every order.

When I update the record, I also review artwork and disposal instructions that depend on it. I check whether the same evidence still applies. If a detail is no longer confirmed, I hold the affected wording until the reviewer resolves it. I retain the previous version for stock that is still in circulation.

## Conclusion

I document every component and attachment, link the record to the accepted box, and verify any change before relying on the same evidence.

[^2]: [4evergreen Circularity by Design guideline](https://4evergreenforum.eu/about/circularity-by-design-guideline/) discusses design considerations for paper-based packaging.
