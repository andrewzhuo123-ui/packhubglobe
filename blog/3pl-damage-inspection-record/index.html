Shipping & protection

How do I record packaging damage at my 3PL?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

My warehouse can send a photo of a crushed carton without showing which item shipped in it. I need enough context to investigate the damage.

I give my 3PL a damage inspection record that links each affected unit to its shipment, SKU, lot and pack configuration. I collect outside and inside photos, count inspected units and record observations before deciding on disposition or a packaging change.

I set up the record before the delivery arrives. I want the receiving team to capture evidence while the package and contents are still together.

What do I ask the 3PL to record at receiving?

A carton can lose its label and packing context as soon as the team starts sorting. I agree on the receiving sequence before that happens.

I record the shipment ID, carton label, SKU and lot where available, receipt time, inspection scope and visible condition. I take photos before opening and tag the affected carton so later photos stay connected.

I define the unit I am counting

I distinguish the shipment, outer shipping carton, retail pack and individual product. If a carton holds twelve products and I see one corner dent, I record a dented carton and inspect the products separately. I avoid calling all twelve units damaged without seeing them. I capture a readable internal carton ID on the photo sheet and map it to the inbound order. When there is a privacy concern, I keep the original with restricted access and redact names or street addresses from the version shared more widely.

I record the scope before opening

I specify whether the inspection is every carton or only a sample. If the team opened six visibly damaged cartons, I record that the sample was selected for visible damage. Its results cannot estimate the condition of the full shipment. For a better comparison later, I ask the 3PL to note how they picked cartons and whether they included intact-looking ones. I record when cartons were already opened or repacked before the photos. That gap changes what I can learn about the original arrangement and how strongly I can connect the observed condition to a stage of handling.

Which photos help me connect the carton to the damage?

A tight photo of a crack shows the defect, but it hides the product position and the nearby cushioning. I request a sequence.

I photograph the labelled exterior, damaged area, first view after opening, packing layers and affected unit. I include a reference for scale and keep the carton tag visible in key frames.

I take the photos in unpacking order

I begin with the full carton and its tag, followed by the side, corner or seam that shows the issue. For the first interior photo, I ask the receiver to lift the flap without removing the contents. If the product moved or the insert buckled, I want that first view to show the as-found position. I then remove one layer at a time and photograph the side of the product that faced the damaged area. I include a ruler where size matters and record the orientation in text. I mark if the label or carton damage was already present before opening.

I keep relevant parts together

I ask the receiver to retain the outer carton, inserts, wraps and affected product where safe and appropriate. If they need to move a damaged item, I ask them to note where it went and whether it was handled after the initial photos. I capture a comparable undamaged unit when possible so I can distinguish a normal fold from a failure. The guide to inserts and transit testing helps me choose what to show for contact, movement and edge protection.

UPS packaging guidance describes securing items and filling empty space to limit movement.1 I use that as a prompt to document gaps and displaced components. I treat the photos as evidence of the received condition, then investigate the packing method and distribution route before assigning a cause.

How do I describe damage without assuming its cause?

A label such as “carrier damage” can close the discussion before anyone has checked the evidence. I write observations and hypotheses separately.

I classify observed damage by layer and location, record inspected and affected counts, and distinguish a suspected mechanism from a confirmed finding. I use the record to decide what evidence is still missing.

I describe the layer and the location

I use separate fields for carton, internal protection, retail packaging and product. For one example, I might write “Outer carton lower right corner crushed, right insert wing flattened, product rim chipped.” On another carton I might see a clean exterior with a loose product and scuff marks inside. Each condition points to a different follow-up question. I ask whether the same damage appears in the same position across more than one carton and compare the available packing photos. The inner and outer dimensions guide gives me a way to record where clearance was measured if fit is part of the hypothesis.

I make the counts comparable

I report damage at the same level as my inspection denominator. If I inspected twelve products and found two cracked, I record two of twelve inspected products. I also report how many cartons those products came from. I describe how they were selected before comparing the result with another receiving event. For example, six cartons chosen because they appeared damaged should not be compared directly with six picked from a mixed lot.

I keep disposition separate as well. A dented retail carton may need rework while the product inside can still be evaluated. The buyer and warehouse can agree what to hold, inspect or release based on the product and their policies. I record who decided, which units it applied to and where the affected material went.

How do I turn receiving evidence into a packaging decision?

A damage report can disappear into email without changing the next shipment. I use the record to select a check and assign an owner.

I identify the most useful next check, compare the pack with its approved specification, and record any change as a new revision. I compare future receiving evidence using the same inspection method.

I select a check that could change the decision

If every affected product shifted away from its intended cavity, I ask the packer to assemble the approved configuration on the bench and record where the insert seats. If the received carton is larger than the approved version, I compare the drawings and ask when the substitution occurred. The mailer sizing guide explains how to consider fit, packing method and delivery requirements together. I do not reduce cushioning solely because the box can be made smaller.

I define how I will evaluate a proposed change

I specify the packed product, carton, insert, closure and packing sequence before requesting any test. FedEx's testing instructions provide an example of preparing the packaged article and following the test with customer inspection where needed.2 I decide with the provider which procedure fits the route and what condition the product must meet afterward. An informal comparison at the 3PL can help me identify a packaging difference, while a formal test has its own configuration and acceptance criteria.

For the next receipt I retain the same photo sequence and identify the revised pack on the record. I keep the sample scope and product mix visible so a change in the inspection method does not masquerade as a change in damage. If the result remains uncertain, I record that and identify the next evidence to gather before expanding the change across the range.

Conclusion

I link receiving photos, counts and pack revisions in one record. That gives the 3PL and buyer evidence they can use for the next decision.
