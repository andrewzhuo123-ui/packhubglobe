Pouches & food packaging

When does a flat pouch work for a single-serve food sample?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can choose a sample pouch that looks right while empty and discover that the portion fouls the seal. I start with the filled pack.

I use a flat pouch for a single-serve food sample when the actual portion fits with room to fill and seal, the recipient can open and empty it, and the material suits the food, process and distribution route.

When I compare options for a flat pouch for a food sample, I start with the portion before I look at catalogue dimensions. I check the fill, seal, opening and approval evidence in that order.

How do I size a flat pouch for the actual portion?

A weight printed on the order tells me how much product goes in. It does not tell me how much room the product needs.

I fill a sample with the real serving, measure the usable space between its seals and check how much clearance remains above the product. I verify the filled thickness and any outer-carton fit.

I start with the portion and its shape

I bring the intended product to the trial. A coarse cereal mix, a fine powder and several irregular pieces can occupy different amounts of space at the same weight. I prepare the serving with the same tools and method planned for production. If the product settles after a short wait, I record the condition at filling as well as the condition after settling. I label the sample with the serving mass and pouch revision so the result stays tied to an actual test.

I ask which dimensions on the drawing describe the outside of the pouch and which describe the open cavity. Side and bottom seals take up part of the footprint. If the package has a notch or other opening feature, I make sure the product will not sit in the path the customer needs to tear. The stand-up and flat-bottom comparison helps me separate the need for a standing display from the need for enough usable space.

I check where the sample will go

I place the filled pouch in its intended display, parcel or kit. A pouch that slides into an envelope while empty may form a thick spot once filled. I check whether product pieces press on the outer pack or prevent a carton from closing. I compare an actual sample in both formats if a flat pouch and a stand-up pouch look plausible. I keep the intended packing orientation with the specification.

Can the filling team keep the sealing area clear?

A portion can fit the pouch and still leave residue near its opening. I test the actual way the product enters the pouch.

I run a representative fill and seal trial with the proposed material and equipment. I check the seal area before closure, record the trial settings and agree on test methods and acceptance criteria.

I watch the fill and seal sequence

I ask the filling team to show how the pack is held open, how the portion enters and how the pouch moves to the sealing point. With dry particles, I pay attention to residue on the inner faces near the top. With irregular pieces, I look for a corner or hard edge caught near a seal. I record when and where an issue occurs. A good result from a carefully filled hand sample may be useful for fit, but it does not show that a production process will give the same result.

The rollstock and premade pouch comparison helps me describe what the equipment needs to accept. A trial should use the actual format, orientation and closure sequence planned for the run. I keep the sample IDs and process notes together so the team can recreate the setup if a supplier changes the material or dimensions.

I distinguish strength from integrity

ASTM F88/F88M describes measuring the force needed to separate a strip containing a flexible seal and noting the mode of failure.1 I ask a qualified team whether that method and a separate integrity check suit my package. A single strong strip does not describe every point around the pack. I agree where specimens come from, how results are recorded and what the buyer will consider acceptable. I leave the numeric targets to the product and test specification rather than borrowing a number from an unrelated pouch.

How will someone open and empty the sample?

A compact sample can look tidy in a kit and frustrate the recipient when it tears poorly. I test opening with the filled pouch.

I ask someone to open the pouch without coaching, observe the tear path and check whether the intended portion comes out without avoidable spilling or product retention. I review the instructions on the finished panel.

I observe the first attempt

I hand the filled pouch to someone who has not watched the sample being packed. I ask them to open it where they would normally use the product. I notice whether they can find the intended starting point, whether the tear runs where expected and whether they need scissors. I also watch the way the portion leaves the pack. A broad opening may help with larger pieces, while a narrower controlled opening may suit a product that needs to pour into a cup. I select the opening around the use case rather than assuming one shape suits both.

I document the difference between the unopened trial pouch and the sample after opening. If a label covers the notch, I move the label or revise the artwork and repeat the check. If the tear removes an instruction the customer needs later, I relocate that information to a panel that stays with the pack. When the sample is packed inside another carton, I also confirm that the recipient can identify the flavor or variant once they remove it.

I try normal handling variations

I include a pouch that has been carried with other items or has been placed in its planned retail kit. I check whether folds obscure the opening cue or whether the product collects on one side. I record the type of issue and the sample involved. That makes the feedback more actionable than a general request for a package that feels easier to use.

What evidence do I need before approving the pouch?

A sample can pass a size and opening check while leaving the material question unanswered. I keep these approvals separate.

I document the exact food, process, storage and distribution conditions, then ask for evidence tied to the proposed material and market. I release production only when the fit, seal, use and material checks are resolved.

I make the use conditions explicit

I write down the food type, fill temperature, contact duration, storage environment and destination market. I include whether the pouch will be used as a short-lived promotional sample or stored longer under a defined condition. In the United States, FDA materials distinguish food types and conditions of use when describing food contact substances.2 I use that distinction to make the supplier request specific. I ask a qualified reviewer to check whether the evidence applies to the exact construction and intended use.

Decision What I attach to the approval
Portion fit Filled sample ID, actual mass and usable dimensions
Seal Trial setup, selected method and agreed result
Opening Photographs or notes from filled-sample attempts
Intended use Food, fill conditions, storage and market
Artwork Approved revision and readable variant information

I keep the order tied to the tested pack

I use the packaging quote checklist to give the supplier a common record of format, intended use, production requirements and requested evidence. If the film, seal geometry or portion changes, I ask which check the change affects before reusing the approval. I keep the approval alongside the physical sample and the test notes. This gives the filling team something specific to compare during the first production run, especially if a plain sample and a printed sample look similar while using different material constructions.

Conclusion

I approve a flat food sample pouch after checking the real portion, filling process, opening and evidence for its intended use.
