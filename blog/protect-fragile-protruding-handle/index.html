Shipping & protection

How should I protect a product with a fragile protruding handle?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can cushion a mug and still break its handle if the insert pushes on the handle when the parcel lands.

I support the product at suitable points on its body and leave a pocket around the fragile protruding handle. I check that the pocket stays clear after packing and handling, then test the complete parcel for its shipping route.

I start by tracing how a load could reach the handle. That shows where to change the support and what to inspect when I open the test parcel.

Which parts of the product should carry the load?

An insert can fit the outline of a product closely and still put its weight on the weakest feature. I choose support points first.

I ask the product team where the item can be supported. I use those areas to restrain the body and leave space around the handle and its attachment points.

I mark the contact points on a real item

I set the product on the bench and identify the surfaces that can carry the load in the intended orientation. On a mug, the base may be a useful starting point. I still look at the rim, any decorative coating and the area around each end of the handle. I ask the product team where pressure or rubbing is acceptable. If the finish is soft, a surface that can carry weight might still need protection from visible marks.

I draw the likely contact points on a photo. Then I place the item in the proposed support and check whether those points match the actual fit. If a pocket grips the handle, I ask whether the body could move against that grip during handling. I avoid relying on the handle as the feature that stops rotation. I try a shaped feature on the body or a separate support that prevents rotation without grabbing the handle.

I use the guide to protective inserts to compare possible materials and shapes. I also check how the customer will lift the item out. If a tight cradle makes the handle the only convenient grip, I add access around a suitable part of the body or change the removal sequence. A pack can protect a handle in transit and still make it easy to damage during unpacking.

How much space do I leave around the handle?

A clearance that looks generous on a drawing can disappear when the product rotates, the insert shifts, or a wall bows.

I check the gap around the handle on real samples, including the largest expected projection. I account for movement, folded material and the space needed to load and remove the product.

I check the pocket in the complete pack

I compare several representative items when the product shape can vary. I identify the one with the greatest handle projection or the handle closest to an insert edge. I put it into a complete carton and check the gap at the closest points. I look beside, above and below the handle. I inspect the surrounding folds and the lid. I use the guide to internal and external box dimensions to make sure the drawing gives the clear space I can actually use.

Area What I check
Tip of the handle Distance from the nearest wall when the body is seated
Handle attachments Clearance from the ends of the support
Lid and flap overlap Gap when the carton is closed normally
Loading and removal path Space for the handle to pass without snagging

I look for routes by which the handle could reach one of those surfaces. I tilt a sample gently to observe where movement stops. I check whether the whole insert slides inside the carton. If the insert can move, I secure it or change the fit before relying on a gap measured at rest. I avoid choosing a universal clearance number because the required space depends on the product variation, the support and how the box is handled.

How do I compare two insert designs before ordering?

A firm insert can limit movement but mark the body. A soft insert can protect the finish but allow the handle to reach the wall.

I compare both designs with the same product and outer carton. I look at body movement, handle clearance, contact marks and the time it takes to pack and unpack each sample.

I run the same packing sequence for both designs

I try two layouts that address the same risk. For example, I might compare a folded board cradle with a shaped insert that locates the body at two heights. I check the space around the handle with each layout. I ask the packer to follow the intended sequence and record where a part catches or needs to be forced. I watch whether the product stays in the intended orientation without extra adjustment.

FedEx's guide to packing breakables describes cushioning and ways to limit movement in the shipping box.1 I apply the same principles to the complete configuration. If the product is supplied in a presentation box, I include its actual outer shipping carton and protection in the trial. I look at the clearance inside the presentation pack as well as the fit of that pack in its outer carton. A well fitted outer carton cannot fix a handle that already bears against an internal divider.

I check each sample immediately after packing, then again after opening. I use a clean product surface for comparison. If I see a scratch on the body, I record where the insert touched it before choosing the firmer option. I use the guide to packaging sample types to request a production-representative version once the layout works on the bench.

How do I verify that the handle stays protected in transit?

I can find plenty of space in a stationary sample and still have a problem after the parcel experiences vibration or impact.

I test the complete packed item under conditions appropriate to the route. I inspect the handle, its attachments, the contact areas and any sign that the support moved.

I write the inspection sequence before testing

I photograph the product and the insert before the test. I record the body orientation, closure method and exact part versions. I set separate criteria for a broken handle, cracks at the joints, cosmetic marks and visible deformation of the support. I ask the quality team how to inspect the handle safely if the material can develop a crack. I keep a sample of the original layout so the team can compare marks in the same locations.

I give the test provider the shipment type, product weight and outer carton details. ISTA lists Procedure 3A for eligible individual packaged products sent through parcel delivery.2 I ask which test applies to my route. I also include different packed configurations when the same item ships alone and in a bundle. A heavier bundle can shift or compress an insert differently.

After testing, I open the parcel in the normal order and photograph the product before moving it. I inspect the handle and the areas that held the body. I check for folds that have collapsed, marks inside the handle pocket and movement at the base. If the handle fails, I change the feature most closely tied to the evidence and repeat the check. When a version passes, I retain the sample record and the exact packing instructions for the warehouse.

Conclusion

I support the body, preserve clearance around the handle, and verify the complete pack through handling and a test suited to the shipping route.
