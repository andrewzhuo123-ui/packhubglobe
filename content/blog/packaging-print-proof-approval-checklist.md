# What should I check before approving a packaging print proof?

I can miss a small file error and repeat it across a full print run. I use a written packaging print proof approval checklist.

**I check the proof's version, dimensions, copy, images, color expectations and production layers before approval. I confirm what the proof can represent and list corrections clearly. I approve one identified file, while keeping structural fit and transport performance on their own approval records.**

I review the proof in four passes so visual preference does not hide a technical error. I keep each pass tied to something I can inspect or confirm.

## Am I reviewing the correct file and dimensions?

I can spend an hour checking the wrong revision. I confirm the file identity and drawing before I look at color or decoration.

**I match the proof to the current technical drawing, artwork version, product variant and finished size. I check panel orientation and the supplier's defined trim, fold and seal areas. I stop approval when the proof and accepted structure do not match.**

### I establish a single review reference

I record the file name, revision and proof date at the top of my checklist. I also identify the product, size and artwork variant. I do not approve a screenshot that has lost its connection to the final print file. I want the supplier and reviewer to point to the same object when they discuss a correction.

I compare the drawing number with the accepted structural sample. I inspect the front, back, side panels and opening direction. I check whether the outside and inside artwork appear on the intended faces. A flat layout can look reasonable while a logo lands upside down when the box is assembled. I fold a paper reference when that helps reveal the sequence.

### I check the production boundaries

| My proof item | My verification | My approval record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drawing | I match the accepted revision | I save its identifier |
| Size | I check scale and units | I note the dimension convention |
| Orientation | I inspect the assembled view | I mark each panel |
| Boundaries | I review trim, folds and seals | I record unresolved conflicts |

I use the [packaging print proof guide](/guides/packaging-print-proof/) to distinguish proof types. I ask the printer to identify what a particular proof represents. A digital PDF, a physical print proof and a finished sample may each answer different questions. I do not let one approval silently cover all three.

## Is every word and code readable on the final pack?

I can approve correct words at an unreadable size. I review the copy in its finished position, not only at a large screen zoom.

**I check spelling, variant details, required product information and the destination of every code or URL. I inspect the layout at final size and on the assembled pack. I verify barcode requirements with the appropriate standard and distinguish a phone scan from formal barcode verification.**

### I review copy as product information

I read the text separately from the design. I compare product names, net contents, variant identifiers and instructions with the approved source copy. I give any market-specific mandatory information to a qualified reviewer. I do not treat the printer's proof as a legal review of the product label.

I look at small type on the actual surface and background. I check whether folds or seals interrupt a sentence. I also review the contrast of light type on a colored area. I do not make every line smaller to fit a crowded panel. I ask which optional copy can be removed while the essential information stays clear.

### I check code use and print quality

GS1 explains that barcode readability depends on features such as clear surrounding space, size and contrast.[^1] I confirm the relevant requirements for the code and use case. I keep decorations, fold lines and other graphics out of the required clear area. I do not assume a stylish code still works because it resembles the original.

I scan a printed sample with the intended devices as a practical check, and I verify the destination page myself. I also ask for proper barcode verification where the sales channel requires it. A single phone scan can show that one device read one sample, but it does not establish full retail compliance. I keep those evidence levels separate in the approval record.

## What can this proof tell me about color and finish?

I can expect a screen proof to show a physical foil or textured board accurately. I want to agree on the limit before I approve it.

**I ask which colors and finishes the proof can represent and which need a physical reference. I compare the approved color target on the agreed material and process. I keep foil, embossing, varnish and white-ink layers clearly identified for production review.**

### I define a usable color target

I ask whether the job uses process colors, spot colors or both. I identify the reference that the printer will use for approval. I do not ask for a perfect match to an uncalibrated phone screen. I also consider the effect of paper shade and surface finish when I compare a printed result.

I keep any agreed color tolerance and viewing condition in the project record. I do not invent a numerical tolerance that the printer has not accepted or that the proof cannot support. If several packaging items must look coordinated, I review them together and identify where different materials will affect the result.

### I inspect the production layers

Adobe's output guidance distinguishes print marks, bleed and color settings.[^2] I use those settings through the printer's specifications. I check that bleed artwork extends where needed and that important content stays inside the agreed safe area. I do not apply one generic bleed number to every structure.

I ask the printer to review separate finish layers and their naming. I check whether a foil, emboss or varnish element overlaps a fold or small type. I identify the areas that need a physical sample before approval. I also confirm that technical lines will not print as visible artwork. I want a clear proof record that describes both the expected appearance and the production instructions needed to achieve it.

## How should I send corrections and final approval?

I can send scattered comments that leave the printer guessing which file is final. I collect the decisions into one clear review record.

**I number corrections, identify their exact locations and request a revised proof. I review the entire revised file, including areas I did not ask to change. I approve one named revision in writing and list any approval that remains separate or unresolved.**

### I make corrections easy to verify

I describe the current issue and the required result. I use a panel name and marked location rather than a vague request to move the logo a little. I collect comments from the relevant reviewers before I send them. I do not let several independent email threads create competing directions for the same panel.

I compare the revised proof with the previous version. I verify every requested change and also look for accidental changes elsewhere. I check font substitutions, image links, variant text and code destinations again. I do not assume an export preserves everything because the visible correction looks right on the first page.

### I close the approval loop

I save the accepted file, drawing reference, approval date and open limitations together. I identify who approved copy and who approved production details. I keep a physical reference where the job depends on a material or finish that the PDF cannot show. I ask the supplier to confirm receipt of the same approved version.

I use the [artwork preparation guide](/guides/prepare-packaging-dielines/) before the next submission and the [structural versus printed sample workflow](/blog/structural-or-printed-packaging-sample/) when fit or material evidence is still missing. I require a new review if the structure, material or artwork changes. That gives the production team a specific approved reference and gives me a clear basis for checking the delivered print.

## Conclusion

I approve a named proof against clear checks. I keep copy, color, production layers and physical-sample limits visible before printing starts.

[^1]: [GS1: 2D barcodes at retail implementation guidance](https://ref.gs1.org/guidelines/2d-in-retail/1.1.0/) — requirements depend on the symbol and intended scanning use.
[^2]: [Adobe: PDF marks, bleeds and color output](https://helpx.adobe.com/illustrator/desktop/save-and-export/export-files-to-different-formats/pdf-marks-bleeds-and-color-output-options.html) — I use the printer's job-specific settings.
