# How do I identify the worst-case parcel in a mixed order range?

I can test the heaviest bundle and miss the light single item that slides across the box and strikes the lid.

**I identify a worst-case parcel for each relevant failure mode. I compare real order recipes for movement, concentrated pressure, closure strain and surface damage. I test distinct configurations when one sample cannot cover all those risks.**

I start with the orders the warehouse can actually ship. Then I narrow the range using a bench comparison before agreeing on formal tests.

## Which orders should I put in the comparison?

A list of product sizes can hide the way the warehouse substitutes boxes or combines items. I start with its packing instructions.

**I list the actual combinations of products, carton sizes, inserts and closures that can go out. I include approved substitutions and partial loads when they change the way an item is protected.**

### I build a short list of genuine parcel recipes

I ask the team for representative single orders, common bundles and the largest combination it allows in each box. I record which instructions the packer follows when a preferred box is unavailable. If the substitute uses a spacer, I include that spacer. I leave unauthorized configurations out of the test scope and record them separately if I see them in practice. That keeps an accidental packing choice from becoming part of the approved range.

I assemble the shortlisted parcels. I note the weight, the space around each item and any point where a rigid part touches a softer one. I include leaflets and protective parts when they affect fit. I record the dimensions after closure, using the [guide to inside and outside dimensions](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) to keep the packed measurements separate from the carton specification.

I check whether each configuration uses the same outer packaging and route. A product that ships alone as a parcel and also travels in a master case belongs in two rows. I make the distinctions early so a later report can show which pack it describes. I do not assume that testing a larger box covers a smaller box made with a different closure.

## How do I rank the configurations by failure mode?

One parcel can put the greatest load on the base while another leaves the most room for movement. I rank those separately.

**I identify the physical feature that makes each failure more likely, then find the parcel that puts the greatest demand on it. I keep ties or unclear cases visible for review.**

### I compare the load where it reaches the packaging

| Failure mode | What I compare |
| --- | --- |
| Item strikes a wall | Free travel in each direction |
| Hard part marks a softer item | Contact area, position and relative movement |
| Base or support deforms | Weight and where the load reaches the support |
| Flap or seal opens | Loaded lid clearance and closure strain |

I check weight distribution as well as total weight. A heavy bundle can spread its weight across several cells. A lighter bottle may apply it to one small point. I photograph the position of the contact and show it to the team. I use the [protective insert guide](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) when I need to describe how the support locates the product or carries the load.

I also look for combinations that change during handling. A tall item might stand clear of a divider on the bench and lean into it if the base moves. I note the possible path and compare it with another recipe. I avoid assigning numerical risk scores that imply a precision I do not have. I record the measured differences and the reason I chose one parcel over the other.

I ask a colleague to review the shortlist and look for an overlooked failure. If two examples challenge different parts of the box, I keep both until I can explain why one result would cover the other.

## What can I learn before sending samples for formal testing?

A simple comparison can reveal a weak closure or a loose insert. I use it to choose samples and refine the questions for the lab.

**I build the likely worst cases on the bench, inspect fit and closure, and compare how the contents move. I use those observations to select test configurations and document their differences.**

### I record observations the lab can use

I check the parcel before handling. I photograph the item positions and mark the carton and insert versions. I close each box using the normal process and compare any bulge or strain. I look for an item that can move into an empty cell or a pad that can lift at one edge. I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to keep the opening and inspection sequence consistent.

I ask a test provider what information it needs to select the procedure. ISTA's 3A overview describes a procedure for eligible individual packaged products in parcel distribution.[^1] I provide the packed size, weight and route and let the provider confirm what applies. I distinguish the bench observation from a formal result when I share the shortlist. A hand tilt can show movement and does not reproduce distribution forces.

I agree on the number and condition of samples before dispatch. FedEx's lab instructions illustrate how a testing provider can specify intake and preparation requirements.[^2] I follow the chosen lab's instructions and make sure each sample has a clear reference. I state which risk the sample was selected to test and include photos or packing directions when the lab needs to reproduce it.

## How do I record which orders the results cover?

A result for one packed bundle can be quoted later as approval for the whole range. I keep the scope visible next to the finding.

**I record the tested configurations, their routes and the reasons for including other orders within the result. I identify changes that require a fresh review or test.**

### I retain a map from the test to the packing instruction

I list the box, insert, item mix and closure for each tested sample. I record the failure mode it was meant to challenge. Then I list the other configurations I believe it represents and explain the comparison. For example, I might group loads that use the same support and have less free travel than the tested sample. If a variant changes the shape or surface at a contact point, I flag it for its own review.

I keep the results separate when different samples cover different risks. If the heaviest bundle passes a base check and a smaller parcel fails for movement, I revise the smaller recipe. I avoid applying one approval to both while the other issue remains open. I tell the warehouse which product and box combinations it can use and record any exception.

I review the scope when a new item is added, a box changes, or the warehouse adopts a different substitute. I check whether that change introduces a heavier concentrated load, greater clearance or a more vulnerable finish. I retain the earlier decision so I can identify which comparison needs repeating. The range can grow without losing the evidence behind each packing instruction.

## Conclusion

I choose worst cases by failure mode, verify representative parcels, and document exactly which orders the results support.

[^1]: [ISTA Procedure 3A overview](https://ista.org/docs/3Aoverview.pdf) describes a general simulation procedure for eligible parcel shipments.
[^2]: [FedEx packaging testing application instructions](https://www.fedex.com/en-us/shipping/packaging/testing/application-instructions.html) describes sample preparation for its testing service.
