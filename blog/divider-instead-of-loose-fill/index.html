Shipping & protection

When should I use a divider instead of loose fill?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can fill a box until it feels snug and still find two products touching after the material shifts.

I use a divider when products need separate positions that the packer can reproduce. I use loose fill for remaining voids when the material is suitable for the load and testing shows that it stays effective.

I decide what must be separated before I choose what will fill the space. Then I compare the complete packing methods on the same orders.

What job do I need the packing material to do?

A gap between products can disappear during handling. Filling space and keeping two items apart are separate jobs.

I choose a divider when I need a physical boundary between products. I choose a suitable void fill when I mainly need to limit movement in the space around an already protected item.

I name the contact that would damage the order

I put the planned order on the bench and identify what can move and which surfaces can be damaged. A metal lid beside a printed carton creates a different risk from a single padded item in an oversized shipping box. If I need to keep those surfaces apart, I check whether a divider can hold each item in its own position. If the items are already individually protected, I check whether the remaining gap allows the whole load to shift.

I use the guide to protective inserts to compare ways of supporting the product. A divider can separate items without cushioning them against the outer walls. I check the space around the individual cells and the way the products sit inside them. I also inspect how the weight reaches the bottom of the carton. A thin wall between products may help with rubbing and do little to support a heavy item.

FedEx's packing guidance for breakables discusses wrapping items and using cushioning to limit movement.1 I translate that into a packing recipe for the actual order. I avoid treating any material as suitable just because it fills the visible space. I check the product weight, shape and surfaces before choosing what sits beside it.

When does a fixed divider work better on the packing bench?

A packing team can learn to place loose fill carefully. Repeating that placement can take time when orders are similar and arrive all day.

I favor a divider when order combinations repeat and each item fits an obvious cell. I verify that the divider stays seated and that the packer can detect a missing or misplaced item.

I compare how different people assemble the same order

I ask two packers to work from the same instructions. I compare where the items end up and how long the work takes. If the divider has a right and wrong orientation, I check whether the shape makes that obvious. I inspect the first assembled box from each person. A divider that looks simple on its own can be difficult to fit when the carton walls are already loaded.

I compare the same checks on a loosely filled pack. I record the amount used and whether the material sits in the intended position. I use the box dimensions guide to show the converter where the divider reduces usable space. If the cells require a larger outer carton, I compare that parcel with the existing box. I also ask the team to stage a shift's supply of dividers. That helps me see whether the design needs more shelf space or another assembly step.

When should I keep loose fill or combine the two?

A fixed layout can waste time when the warehouse ships a different mix in every carton. I check whether the parts need precise locations.

I keep loose fill for suitable variable gaps where products are already separated or protected. I combine it with a divider when the divider controls contact but a remaining void still allows movement.

I check the void after the important parts are secure

I place the items in their intended positions. Then I look for space around them, including the gap under the lid. If a divider gives each item a cell but the full assembly can slide, I check whether a fitted pad or a measured amount of fill will hold it. I avoid using loose material as a substitute for a divider that never seats properly. I correct the support first so I can see what the extra material actually contributes.

UPS's packaging guidelines describe cushioning and preparation for package shipments.2 I check the guidance for the service and choose a material suited to the order. If a heavy item compresses or displaces the material, I look for a support that stays in position. I also check whether small pieces of fill become trapped inside a product opening or interfere with the way the customer lifts it out.

I specify the quantity and location of fill in the packing recipe. A direction to fill all gaps can leave different packers with different results. I use a marked photo and compare the normal closed parcel after each trial. If one order needs much more material than expected, I consider another box size or a separate packing instruction.

How do I choose after the handling trial?

A divider can cost more per order and save time at the bench. I compare the accepted packs rather than the price of each part alone.

I test representative orders using each method and inspect movement, contact marks and ease of opening. I compare material, labor and shipping costs for the options that pass.

I compare the same loads under the same conditions

I select order combinations that are likely to reveal differences. I include a heavy load and a smaller load with more room to move when both use the same box. I record the product orientation, amount of fill, divider version and closure. I use a handling plan suited to the shipment and photograph the contents before removing them. The warehouse damage inspection guide helps me record the condition in a repeatable sequence.

I look for contact between products and for any new marks on their surfaces. I check whether the divider folded, the fill compressed or the whole pack moved. If both methods pass the agreed checks, I compare their costs. I time the complete packing process and include any difference in storage and packed parcel size. I also note the time needed to clear loose material from the bench when that is part of the normal work.

I retain the chosen recipe and identify the orders it covers. If a new product changes the weight or leaves a cell empty, I check the result again. I keep the alternative method available only for configurations it can protect. That gives the packing team a clear choice when the order mix changes.

Conclusion

I use a divider for repeatable separation, fill suitable remaining gaps, and choose the method that protects the actual order at an acceptable cost.
