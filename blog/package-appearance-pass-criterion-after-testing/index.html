Shipping & protection

How do I set a pass criterion for package appearance after testing?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can approve a transport test and still disagree with receiving about whether a marked carton is fit to sell.

I define the surfaces, defects and limits before testing. I agree on how to inspect the pack, keep examples of acceptable and unacceptable marks, and report appearance separately from product function and shipping-carton condition.

I start from the point at which someone sees or uses the pack. That helps me set a different limit for a display panel than for a hidden flap.

Which surfaces should have their own acceptance criteria?

A small mark on a shipping case can be routine. The same mark over a product name can make the retail pack unsuitable for display.

I divide the pack into zones by how people see and use them. I set separate checks for display surfaces, opening features, hidden areas and the outer shipping container.

I identify the surfaces in the order the customer sees them

I put the pack in its intended display position and photograph the front, sides and top. I mark which faces are visible in a tray or on a shelf. Then I open the pack and note what the customer sees first. The inside of a gift box lid may matter more than its bottom panel. I check whether a corner that looks hidden on the drawing becomes visible after the box opens. I save a marked photograph with the sample reference so the reviewer can find each zone without interpreting a flat drawing.

Zone What I assess
Main display face Visible marks, damaged print and loss of legibility
Opening feature Tears, displaced tabs and appearance after opening
Hidden base or inner flap Wear that affects fit or leaves visible debris
Outer shipping carton Condition needed to protect and handle the parcel

I also check which marks could interfere with using the pack. A crease near an opening may affect both appearance and function. I record those outcomes separately. I use the packaging print proof guide to identify the approved finish and visible features. I ask the product owner to confirm the surfaces that matter before I write the defect limits.

How do I turn a visual preference into a usable limit?

Terms like minor scuff can mean different things to a buyer and a lab. I give the reviewer an observable condition and an example.

I describe each defect, the zone it affects and the limit I will accept. I use reference photos or retained samples for cases that are hard to describe with a measurement alone.

I define what crosses the line

I start with the defects I expect to see, such as rubbed ink, a dent, a crease or lifted paper. I decide how to judge each one. For a scuff, I may specify whether it can be seen at the agreed distance or crosses a word. For a dent, I may also record its location and size. I write a limit that the inspection team can apply with the tools and time it has. I avoid adding a numerical tolerance if the team cannot measure it consistently.

I compare borderline examples with the buyer or product owner. I show the same sample to two people and ask them to assess it independently. If they disagree, I ask which part of the instruction led them to a different result. I adjust the language or add a reference image. I keep the acceptable example next to the unacceptable one and mark the exact area being compared. I also date the reference because a sample can pick up marks in storage.

ISTA's Procedure 3A overview calls for defining product damage tolerance and allowable package degradation before testing.1 I use that distinction to write separate acceptance lines. I also decide how to report a sample that passes the cosmetic check but fails a closure or product check. That keeps the overall decision from hiding the individual result.

How should the lab inspect the pack after testing?

A mark can appear when the inspector removes the product. I agree on the viewing conditions and the order of inspection before the test.

I specify lighting, viewing distance, orientation and opening sequence. I ask the inspector to photograph the pack as found, then compare the named zones with the agreed examples.

I keep the inspection order consistent

I record the appearance before the test so the reviewer can distinguish a new mark from an existing print defect. I ask the lab to photograph the outer carton before opening and the inner packs before moving them. I use the warehouse damage inspection guide to align the image sequence with the way our receiving team records damage. If the pack opens in stages, I show which surfaces to photograph at each stage.

I specify what the reviewer should do under normal lighting and what requires a closer view. An enlarged photograph can help trace a mark back to a divider. I use the agreed normal view for the appearance decision when that is what the criterion calls for. If the finish is glossy, I state how the panel is held so glare does not obscure the comparison. I check that the photographs preserve enough detail to show the finding.

I agree on how the lab will record each sample and any change to the inspection method. FedEx provides a post-test form for its packaging lab service.2 I confirm what the selected lab will document and give it the criteria that apply to my pack. I ask for a record of any sample that the team could not inspect in the agreed condition.

How do I report the result when some samples look better than others?

An average can hide a rejected display face. I record each sample and apply the decision rule agreed before testing.

I record each defect by sample and zone. I apply the agreed rule for failures and borderline results, then state exactly which package version and conditions the decision covers.

I keep individual results beside the final decision

I create a row for each sample. I identify the package version, test condition and affected surface. I note whether the mark was visible before testing. I attach the photographs and explain how I applied the criterion. If a result is borderline, I compare it with the reference and send it to the named reviewer. I do not change the threshold simply because the first result is inconvenient.

I keep the report tied to the finished configuration. If I replace a separator or change the orientation, I record the revised version before repeating the check. I use the packaging sample types guide to describe how closely the tested samples match production. I check whether the result can support the order I am about to place, including the finish and case layout it will use.

I give receiving the same photographs and criteria used for the test. I ask the team to flag any damage pattern outside the agreed examples. If the first shipment shows a new pattern, I review the package and the shipment record before updating the criteria. That preserves a useful comparison between the laboratory result and what arrives in practice.

Conclusion

I define appearance limits before the test, inspect each named zone consistently, and preserve individual results with the package version.
