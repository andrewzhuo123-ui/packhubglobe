# When should I ask for a second production supplier?

I can have two packaging quotes and still face a shutdown if only one factory can make the approved pack on time.

**I qualify a second production supplier when a disruption could exhaust my usable stock before I can recover. I check that the alternate can obtain the inputs, make an acceptable pack and deliver within the time I need.**

I start with the item that would stop dispatch first. Then I compare the time I have with the time a second converter would need.

## Which packaging items warrant a second source?

An expensive gift box can be easy to replace. A cheap fitted insert can stop the entire order if I have no approved alternative.

**I prioritize parts that stop sales or filling, have few substitutes, and take longer to replace than my stock can cover. I compare backup qualification with extra stock and simpler pack options.**

### I measure time to shortage by usable component

I list the parts I need to complete an order. I count stock I can use and remove pieces already committed elsewhere. Then I estimate how long that quantity lasts at normal and peak use. If I have 900 approved inserts and use 300 per week, I have about three weeks of coverage at that rate. If usage doubles during a promotion, I have about a week and a half. I check when the next receipt will become available rather than counting every case the supplier says is on its way.

I compare that coverage with the time to recover. I include finding an alternate, reviewing its sample, placing an order, and receiving usable cartons. If a backup needs longer than I have, I start qualification sooner or review whether to carry more stock. I use the [supplier selection guide](/guides/choose-packaging-supplier-china/) to describe the capacity and production checks I need from an alternate converter.

NIST's manufacturing guidance discusses assessing supply chain risks and planning responses.[^1] I use that approach to rank the parts. A plain mailer available from several sources may need only a documented substitute. A carton made with a distinctive finish or a fitted insert may need a working second source before the next launch.

## How do I tell whether the alternate is independent enough?

Two suppliers can buy from the same board mill or use the same finishing plant. I ask what each one would need during the disruption I am planning for.

**I ask which plant will produce the order and where the critical stock, tools and finishing work come from. I compare those dependencies with the failure I need the second source to cover.**

### I trace the shared bottleneck

I ask which operations happen at the converter and which are outsourced. If my main concern is a lost machine slot, a different production line may help. If I am worried that a specialty board will be unavailable, a second converter using the same board may face the same delay. I check whether it stocks the grade, can obtain it from a separate source, or could make a qualified alternative. I document which scenario each plan covers.

| Disruption I am planning for | What I check with the alternate |
| --- | --- |
| Main plant loses a production slot | Available capacity on a suitable line |
| Specialty finish is delayed | Location and availability of finishing work |
| Tool cannot be moved | Access to files and a realistic rebuild time |
| Grade becomes unavailable | Stock in hand or an approved replacement |

FEMA's supply chain resilience guidance provides a framework for understanding supply chain dependencies.[^2] I apply that idea at the part level. I ask for enough detail to distinguish a true alternative from two sales contacts relying on the same operation. I also check whether the alternate would have capacity when other customers are looking for the same help. I do not count a general promise as a reserved slot.

## What should I verify before I rely on a second supplier?

An alternate may produce a good sample by hand and still be unable to repeat it on the intended line. I qualify the steps that matter.

**I give the alternate the approved specification and request a representative sample or trial. I verify fit, appearance, packing performance and the time needed to deliver a usable order.**

### I compare a trial with the existing accepted pack

I send the structural and artwork revisions, approved materials and inspection criteria. I check which tooling or digital files the alternate needs. I use the [dieline preparation guide](/guides/prepare-packaging-dielines/) to keep the structural handoff clear. I ask the alternate to list any difference from the existing pack before it makes the sample. A similar looking board may require a separate review if the grade supports a customer requirement.

I compare the finished samples with the current version. I load the actual product and inspect the points that matter for assembly and display. If the pack runs on equipment, I verify the proposed carton in that process. I use the [guide to packaging sample types](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to identify what the chosen sample can demonstrate. Where a sample cannot establish repeatability, I ask for a small representative run and a clear record of the process.

I also check delivery from order release through receipt. I ask when the converter needs the deposit, stock and approvals. I confirm how the pieces will be packed and identified. When the trial arrives, I compare its actual timing and accepted quantity with the plan. I keep any unresolved difference visible before naming the alternate as a backup.

## How do I keep the backup ready without buying too much stock?

A supplier I qualified last year may have changed equipment or lost the stock it used for the sample. I decide how to keep its status current.

**I set a review date and a clear trigger for moving work. I confirm the alternate's current specification, tool access, capacity and lead time before depending on it.**

### I choose the level of readiness I need

I ask how quickly I would have to switch during the disruption I identified. For one item, keeping files and a recent accepted sample may give me enough time. For a part that can stop next week's orders, I may need a small regular order or a confirmed capacity arrangement. I compare that expense with the cost of holding more approved packaging at the warehouse. I use the same expected usage when comparing the choices.

I record who can request a switch and who approves the first shipment from the alternate. I state which items and revisions it can make. If a new finish or insert enters production with the main supplier, I check whether the backup remains qualified. I update both records when the change affects fit, appearance or a customer requirement.

I review the alternate's contact, delivery estimate and accepted sample before the next peak. If the supplier no longer makes the pack, I update the recovery plan and calculate what stock coverage remains. I also record actual performance from any backup order. I check whether the new source can replenish the next batch as well as the first one. A one-time stock purchase can bridge a short delay while leaving the longer disruption unresolved. That helps me decide whether to keep the arrangement, improve it or qualify another source.

## Conclusion

I qualify a second supplier when stock cannot cover a realistic disruption, then verify that the alternate can deliver approved packaging in time.

[^1]: [NIST guidance on manufacturing supply chain risk](https://www.nist.gov/feature-stories/how-small-manufacturers-can-develop-risk-management-strategies-their-supply-chains) discusses how small manufacturers can assess and respond to supply risks.
[^2]: [FEMA Supply Chain Resilience Guide](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/supply-chain-resilience-guide.pdf) discusses mapping supply chain dependencies and disruptions.
