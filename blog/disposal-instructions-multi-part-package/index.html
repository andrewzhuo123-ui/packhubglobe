How do I write disposal instructions for a multi-part package?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can write one recycling instruction for a box and leave the buyer guessing what to do with the tray, wrap and window.

I name each component the customer handles, check how it separates, and verify the disposal route in the market where I sell it. I place the instructions where the customer can see them before the parts get separated.

I start with a finished pack and follow it from opening to disposal. That shows which decisions the customer needs to make and which parts the supplier needs to identify.

Which components do I name on the package?

A materials list can tell me the box contains paper and plastic. It does not tell me which parts the customer can remove.

I list the parts as the customer sees them, including the box, tray, wrap and any removable window. I keep bonded layers in the technical record and assess them as part of the component they belong to.

I open a finished sample and follow the parts

I lay the pieces on a table in the order they come out. I name each piece in a way a person can match to the item in hand. If I call two different pieces an insert, I add a visible distinction such as paper tray and clear sleeve. I photograph the set and label the photograph for the design team. I keep the photos with the package revision, because a replacement tray can look similar and use a different material.

I ask the supplier to confirm the material of each piece and identify coatings, laminations, adhesives or attachments that affect the disposal assessment. I use the packaging materials guide to align the basic terms in the request. I record the supplier's construction details separately from the words I plan to print. A box may contain an attached window, for example. I first find out how it is attached and whether a person can remove it before deciding whether it needs an instruction of its own.

I also check the package after ordinary use. A product can leave residue on the tray or inside the wrapper. A tab can tear away with the lid. I note the state in which the customer will hold each part. That is the condition I use for the next assessment.

How do I check which route applies to each part?

A supplier may describe a material as recyclable even when the finished component has no confirmed collection route in the sales market.

I verify the finished component against current guidance for the intended market. I record the source, date, conditions and any limits before printing a disposal direction or making a recycling claim.

I keep an evidence row for each customer decision

Component Condition I check Evidence I need
Outer carton Window removed or attached Guidance for the finished construction as discarded
Insert Dry and separate from the product Material and acceptance for the actual tray
Clear sleeve Film type and any attached label Applicable collection route and preparation instructions
Window Whether removal is practical Guidance for the separated piece if removal is required

I write down the regions where the pack will be sold before I choose the wording. If the same pack crosses markets, I check the actual instructions and labeling rules in each destination. I do not assume one area's collection guidance can support a claim everywhere. In the United States, the FTC's Green Guides summary addresses when a marketer should qualify a recyclable claim based on access to appropriate facilities.1 I record the date and scope of the reference and send the proposed wording through the relevant market review.

I distinguish the original material from the component that will reach the collection system. A supplier might identify the main board grade, while the finished carton adds a coating and a glued patch. I ask about the final construction and use the guide to coatings on kraft packaging to frame that question. I also check if size, residue, or an attached piece changes the applicable instruction.

When a route is uncertain, I mark the component for review and keep the proposed claim out of the artwork. I make that hold visible to the designer. A general recycling icon can be read as a statement about the whole pack, so I review symbols and words together.

How do I check that the customer can follow the instruction?

A line that says to remove the window can look simple in a proof. A glued window may tear into pieces when someone tries it.

I give people the closed pack and the proposed instructions. I watch where they look, whether they can separate each named part, and what they think the wording tells them to do.

I test separation in the order it actually happens

I use the pack the customer receives, with the correct tape, seals and inserts. I ask someone unfamiliar with the design to open it without coaching. I watch whether they find the instruction before discarding the outer sleeve. If the text says to lift a tab, I check that they can identify and grip it. If the instruction depends on peeling off a patch, I check what remains on both pieces. A partly removed patch may leave the buyer unsure which direction applies.

I note the difference between a wording issue and a design issue. If the buyer sees two pieces that could both match the term tray, I make the wording more specific. If the tab is difficult to grip, I ask the packaging team to review the structure. I use the dieline preparation guide to show where a new release tab or revised panel could go. I retest the actual sample if that change affects opening or strength.

I also ask the person to point to each part and tell me what they would do with it. A person can successfully separate the pack and still misread which instruction goes with which part. I record that error without correcting it during the trial. Then I adjust the names, order, or placement and repeat the exercise on a new sample.

Where should I put the instructions and how do I keep them current?

A label on the sleeve can disappear before the buyer reaches the inner tray. I check which printed surfaces survive each step.

I put the full instructions where the customer can find them during opening and repeat a component instruction where needed. I tie the artwork approval to the package construction and the markets it covers.

I approve placement on the assembled package

I compare the proof with a physical sample and check the order of the directions. I use short actions and place each action next to the name of its component. I avoid relying on a drawing or color alone when two parts look similar. If the outer panel carries the only direction for an unprinted insert, I make sure the insert is easy to identify from that panel. How2Recycle's published guidelines show placement options for multi-component packs under its program.2 I use the approved process for any program label the brand intends to carry.

I assign someone to review the wording when a material, supplier, adhesive or sales market changes. I ask for a new material record if the supplier makes a substitution. I also schedule a check of the original evidence when artwork comes up for reprint, since acceptance guidance can change. I retain the approval date and market scope with the design file.

Before production, I check the assembled pack for legibility. A direction can sit too close to a seam, disappear under a sticker, or become hard to read on a glossy area. I verify the final position after folding and sealing. When the team can point to the correct text on the packed item and match it to the evidence record, I can approve the release.

Conclusion

I name the actual parts, verify each route, and test the directions on a finished pack before approving disposal instructions.
