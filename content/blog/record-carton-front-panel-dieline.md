# How do I record the intended front panel of a carton on a dieline?

I can approve the logo on a flat layout and find it facing away from the shopper after the carton is folded.

**I mark the front panel, top edge and reading direction on a separate copy of the supplier's dieline. I match those marks to photographs of a formed carton and verify the production artwork against the same structural revision.**

I start with the product as a customer will see it. Then I work backward from the folded carton to the flat drawing.

## How do I identify the front on a formed carton?

Two panels may have the same dimensions. I identify the front by how the carton is filled, displayed and opened.

**I load and close a blank sample, place it in the intended selling position, and mark the panel facing the customer. I also mark the edge I consider the top.**

### I use the actual shelf and opening position

I put the product into the blank sample in the orientation the packer will use. I stand the carton as it will appear in the store. If a display tray covers the lower part of the front, I include that tray in the check. If the carton hangs from a tab, I check which face points outward when it hangs. The same blank can look correct in both orientations on a desk. The selling position gives me a clearer reference.

I put a removable label on the chosen face and draw an arrow toward the top. I mark the side that faces the customer and note which flap the customer opens. I use the [guide to straight and reverse tuck ends](/guides/straight-vs-reverse-tuck-end/) to describe the closure without relying on words like left and right by themselves. Those words can change meaning when the sample is turned over.

I photograph the carton from the front and then from an angle that shows the top and one side. I include the sample reference and name the intended orientation in the record. If a retailer displays it in a different position from the one used in a product photo, I document both and agree which one the artwork should prioritize. That prevents the designer from using the photo alone to infer the front.

## What should I mark on the flat dieline?

A note that says front can land on the right panel with the wrong reading direction. I record both the face and the edge.

**I label the front panel on a separate reference drawing and add an arrow toward its top. I identify the outside print view and mark the neighboring side, closure and glue area as landmarks.**

### I transfer the marks from the sample to the drawing

I open the marked sample carefully so I can follow the crease around the front panel. I compare the unfolded blank with the supplier's drawing. I look for the glue tab, the openings and the tuck flaps before copying the mark. If the drawing and sample seem reversed, I ask whether the drawing shows the outside or inside of the carton. I confirm the viewing side with the converter and put it on the reference.

| Mark on the drawing | What I check on the formed sample |
| --- | --- |
| Front panel | Which face the customer sees |
| Top arrow | Which way the main text reads |
| Adjacent side | Which panel appears beside the front |
| Opening flap | Which edge the customer opens |

Esko describes structural files that carry cut and crease information and may also carry fold angles.[^1] I use the supplier's structure as the reference for those features. I add the orientation notes to a separate review copy and keep the original structural file available. The [dieline preparation guide](/guides/prepare-packaging-dielines/) helps me describe the required handoff.

I make the front mark large enough to see without zooming. I also check that the arrow points to an actual edge rather than to the top of the page. When the artwork is rotated for layout or output, the labeled edge still gives the reader a reference.

## How do I keep the orientation marks out of the printed art?

A helpful arrow on a proof can become an unwanted printed arrow if it is treated as part of the design. I separate the files.

**I keep orientation instructions on a reference copy or in an agreed nonprinting workflow. I ask the printer to confirm that those instructions will be excluded from the production ink layers.**

### I check the handoff files separately

I send a reference view that makes the direction easy to see and a production file prepared to the printer's requirements. I name their purposes in the handoff. I confirm whether the converter wants the technical drawing separately or in an agreed layer of the production file. I ask how the printer will distinguish technical information from the graphics that should appear on the pack. The Ghent Workgroup describes Processing Steps for identifying production information that is not part of the printed design.[^2] I confirm what the converter's workflow supports.

I compare the production artwork with the marked reference before approval. I check that the front art sits between the correct creases and reads toward the top edge. I inspect the artwork after export because a designer may rotate a panel in the working file and overlook how it appears in the final output. I also check any required side copy and make sure the glue area is handled according to the printer's requirements.

I keep the annotated reference and production file under the same release record. If the supplier receives only a production PDF, I send the orientation view with the approval instructions. If it receives an updated reference after the PDF has already been sent, I ask it to confirm which production revision the reference describes.

## What do I verify on the final folded proof?

A front panel can read correctly while the adjacent copy or top flap reads upside down. I review the whole carton in the order a customer sees it.

**I fold and load the proof, place it in the intended display position, and check the front, sides and opening. I approve it against the named dieline and artwork revisions.**

### I follow the view from display to opening

I read the front from the intended angle. I turn the carton toward the adjacent side and check whether the text still reads in the expected direction. Then I open it. If a message is meant to greet the customer after opening, I make sure it faces them when the flap moves. I use the [print proof guide](/guides/packaging-print-proof/) to keep this check tied to the approved files and sample.

I look for differences between carton sizes that use related artwork. A shallow carton can put the tuck flap on a different side or leave less room around the front. I do not assume that the same rotation works on every dieline. I compare a sample of each affected structure and record which front mark belongs to which one. If the converter changes the glue seam or fold layout, I repeat the check using the new sample.

I keep photographs of the folded proof beside the annotated drawing. The production approval names the artwork and dieline revisions and shows how the carton should be displayed. At reorder, I can compare the incoming pack with that record. If a panel reads differently, I can trace the issue back to an exact file or structural change.

## Conclusion

I mark the front and top from a formed sample, keep those marks separate from printed art, and verify the orientation on the folded proof.

[^1]: [Esko guidance on structural design files](https://docs.esko.com/docs/en-us/studioadvanced/23.03/userguide/en-us/common/ste/concept/co_aboutardfiles.html) explains cut, crease and fold information.
[^2]: [Ghent Workgroup Processing Steps](https://gwg.org/technical-specifications/processing-steps/) describes identifying nonprinting production information in PDFs.
