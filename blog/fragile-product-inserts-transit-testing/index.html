Shipping & protection

How do I choose inserts and tests for fragile product shipping?

By PackHubGlobe editorial team · 6 November 2026

I can receive an intact outer box and still find a broken product inside. I need to understand movement and contact before I add more board.

I choose inserts for fragile products by identifying likely damage, controlling movement and protecting vulnerable parts. I test the complete packed product against an agreed shipping profile. I keep fit checks, informal handling checks and formal transport tests as separate kinds of evidence.

I use this process to prepare a testable brief. I want the insert designer, packing team and test provider to assess the same product and failure criteria.

What can the damage pattern tell me?

I may ask for a stronger box when the real problem is two products striking each other. I start with the damage evidence.

I compare product damage, retail-pack damage and outer-carton condition. I record the packing arrangement and the order's route. I use that evidence to form a failure hypothesis, then test it rather than assign blame from one photograph.

I collect the full picture

I ask for photos of the product, all sides of the outer carton and the internal packing before it is discarded. I record the order configuration and identify any missing component. I also note whether the closure opened, whether the insert tore or whether the product left its intended position. Those details can point to different problems.

I separate repeated patterns from isolated marks. A broken pump at the same contact point across several orders may suggest a concentrated load. Broad surface rubbing may suggest movement. I treat those as working hypotheses, not a diagnosis that a photo can prove. I still need to examine the actual product and packing system.

I write a failure map

My observation My first question Evidence I request
Two jars touch Did the divider move? Packed and opened views
A cap breaks Did a load reach the cap? Insert contact points
Retail carton scuffs Did the product rub in transit? Surface and fit detail
Outer box opens Did the closure stay engaged? Closure and tape condition

I keep the tone practical. A Reddit account of a disputed damaged shipment shows how hard responsibility becomes when the evidence is incomplete.1 I do not treat that account as a measured damage rate. I use it to justify a clearer inspection record and a defined packing method before the next shipment.

Which insert material should I compare?

I can choose a material by appearance and miss how it loads the product. I want the material and structure to work together.

I compare paperboard, corrugated board, molded pulp and other proposed cushions against the product's shape and failure risk. I check retention, contact area, removal and packing effort. I request performance evidence for the actual construction instead of relying on the material name.

I define the insert's job

I decide whether the insert must locate a product, separate several items, provide cushioning or present a gift set. Those jobs can overlap, but I do not assume one component does all of them equally well. A presentation tray can hold a jar upright while leaving a fragile neck exposed to force.

I identify the surfaces that can accept support and the surfaces that should avoid pressure. I give the designer the product's weight, dimensions and shape variation. I include any cap, pump or handle. I also ask how the product will be removed, because a tight opening can damage the product label or make the package difficult to use.

I compare practical tradeoffs

I consider a folded board insert when a simple structure can locate the contents and suit flat delivery. I consider a shaped tray when the geometry needs more contact control. I still need a sample in the proposed material. A rough appearance sample cannot establish the behavior of a different production material.

I review box insert options as a starting point for the specification. I ask the supplier to explain tolerances, assembly and the effect of product variation. I also record the number of insert pieces. A multi-part solution may create extra packing steps and more ways to omit a component. I compare the complete workflow before I decide that a more complex insert is justified.

What should the sample test prove?

I can approve a nice-looking sample and still leave the main shipping risk unanswered. I give each test a question before I run it.

I use a fit sample to check loading, retention and removal. I use a suitable transport test to assess the packed product under defined hazards. I agree on acceptance criteria before testing and keep the report tied to the sample revision.

I set observable criteria

I define what counts as failure for this product. I may need to assess function, leakage, cracks, movement or visible retail damage. I avoid a vague instruction such as arrive safely because different reviewers can interpret it differently. I photograph the starting condition so I can compare the package after the test.

I ask the test provider to select a method for the actual distribution system. ISTA lists procedures for different routes and package conditions.2 I do not choose a test only because its name appears on a competitor's website. I explain whether the product travels as an individual parcel, in a master carton or on a pallet, and I discuss any later repacking.

I keep the complete system fixed

I test the product, retail pack, insert, outer box and closure together. I record the board or cushion specification and the packing instruction. If I change the insert material after the test, I do not assume that the previous report covers it. I ask the provider whether a new assessment or test is needed.

I distinguish development trials from a final approval. A failed trial can be useful if it identifies a specific weak point. I revise that part, document the change and repeat the relevant check. I do not keep changing several components at once when I want to understand which change improved the result. That discipline makes the test report useful for future reorders too.

How do I keep protection consistent after approval?

I can approve one careful sample while routine packs drift away from it. I want the dispatch method to remain visible and repeatable.

I save an approved packing instruction with component codes, photos and closure details. I train packers against that reference and review the first production batch. I track damage by pack version so I can investigate changes without losing the history.

I turn the sample into an instruction

I photograph each packing stage and keep the instruction short. I show which face points upward, where a divider locks and how the closure is secured. I identify optional pieces clearly so they are not confused with required protection. I ask a person who did not design the pack to follow the instruction and explain any uncertain step.

I keep the approved product and packaging references together. I note a change in bottle supplier, cap shape or retail-carton size because that change can affect the insert. I do not treat product dimensions as fixed forever. I want purchasing and dispatch to know when a new component requires a fit review.

I use incoming and dispatch feedback

I inspect the first received batch for the agreed features. I check the insert's assembly, the box closure and the complete packed fit. I do not infer that every unit matches from a single good-looking carton. I agree on an inspection plan appropriate to the order and its risk.

I review damage reports with the pack version, shipping service and order mix. I avoid drawing a strong conclusion from a tiny batch or a different route. I use the sample approval guide to keep evidence types clear and the mailer sizing workflow if excessive space contributes to movement. I then make the next revision answer a defined failure, rather than add material without a reason.

Conclusion

I choose inserts around failure risks and test the full packed product. I protect that result with clear approvals, packing instructions and revision records.

How can I right-size a mailer box without losing protection?
