# How do I avoid crushing a soft product under a carton insert?

I can hold a soft product firmly and leave it with a crease that stays visible after the buyer takes it out.

**I support the insert on the carton or other suitable surfaces and keep pressure away from parts of the product that deform. I check the actual closed pack, then measure or inspect the product after an agreed recovery period.**

I separate the need to keep the product in place from the need to carry pressure. An insert can do both jobs, but I check them independently.

## How do I find the point that presses on the product?

A mark on a soft item can come from a ridge in the insert, the lid pressing down, or the way the item was folded.

**I compare the product before packing, just after closure and after the pack has been held or handled. I match the mark with the nearby fold, edge or support.**

### I identify when the deformation first appears

I photograph a representative product before placing it in the carton. I record the way it is folded and the areas that need to keep their shape. For example, a padded textile item may have a raised seam or a decorative surface that shows a pressure line. I put it in the normal position and check whether the insert touches that point before I close the lid. I keep a second sample to compare if opening the first one changes the arrangement.

I close the loaded carton using the ordinary sequence. I note whether the lid needs force or a top pad shifts while the flaps move. I open one sample promptly and inspect the product before unfolding it. If a mark already appears, I check the geometry and assembly first. If it appears only after the pack has been stored or handled, I compare the effects of time and external load.

I keep the original condition and the new mark in the same record. I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to photograph the product before removing the insert. I also note any mark created during opening. That avoids asking the converter to change a surface that did not cause the original problem.

## How do I redirect pressure away from the soft area?

A top insert can restrain an item and carry lid pressure straight into it. I find a different path for the pressure first.

**I give the insert supports that rest on the carton or another approved load-bearing surface. I leave room above sensitive areas and verify that the product cannot move into a new contact point.**

### I check where the insert rests after the box is closed

I examine the insert without the product to see how it sits in the carton. If it rests only on the product once packed, I ask the converter to try folded legs, a ledge, or another geometry that carries pressure elsewhere. I check that those features stay seated and do not bow into the protected area. I use the [guide to protective inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) to compare designs that locate the item while keeping the intended contact points clear.

| What I observe | Change I investigate |
| --- | --- |
| Line across a raised seam | Increase clearance or move the insert edge |
| Broad dent under a pad | Support the pad away from the soft product |
| Mark near a corner | Check side movement and the loading path |
| Deformation only in a stack | Compare the load path through the carton and insert |

I check the revised design with real product samples. I inspect the thickest area and any variation caused by folding or packing. The [guide to inside and outside dimensions](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) helps me communicate the usable interior clearance to the converter. I also look at what happens if the product moves sideways. Extra room above it does little if a hard insert edge can still rub against it during handling.

## How do I judge whether a temporary mark is acceptable?

A soft product may look compressed as soon as it is removed and recover later. I agree on which condition the buyer should see.

**I set an inspection time and a recovery period with the product owner. I compare the shape, appearance and function with an accepted reference under the same conditions.**

### I separate immediate appearance from lasting damage

I ask the product team which changes matter. A shallow impression on a removable wrap might be acceptable. A persistent ridge on a display surface or a part that no longer works may require a hold. I check the product immediately after unpacking and again after the agreed recovery period. I record both observations. I avoid stretching or smoothing the sample unless the instructions call for it. That keeps the result comparable across trials.

I define the condition of the sample when I measure it. For a padded item, I might photograph the affected area from the side and note the height at the same points before and after packing. For a textile item, I might use a visual reference at an agreed viewing distance. I check whether the product can still be used as intended. I record cosmetic and functional findings separately.

If the pack is held for a long time before dispatch, I include a representative holding period in the comparison. I also ask whether expected storage conditions could change the way the product or the insert behaves. I tell the reviewer exactly which conditions were used so the result is not interpreted as a guarantee for every storage arrangement.

## What should I verify under shipping and storage loads?

A clear gap on the bench can close when cartons are stacked or handled. I check the complete shipping unit before releasing the revised pack.

**I test the packed product with its normal outer carton, closure and load. I inspect the supports and product after the agreed handling or compression trial and retain the sample configuration.**

### I compare the approved pack with the revised one

I describe the shipping and storage conditions to the test provider. I include the size and weight of the full carton and explain whether it travels alone or in a case. ASTM D642 describes compression testing for shipping containers, components and unit loads.[^1] I ask whether an applicable compression check can help answer my question and which other handling conditions need to be represented. I do not use a container compression result alone to decide whether the soft product remains acceptable.

ISTA's guidance on selecting procedures explains the connection between testing and the distribution environment.[^2] I give the lab the product condition and the inspection time we agreed. I record any movement of the insert and compare the product with the original reference. If I see a new mark, I look for the closest contact point before changing the design again.

I keep the accepted insert revision, carton and packing sequence in the release record. I specify how the warehouse should place the soft item and which parts should carry the load. At the next order I repeat the affected checks if the product thickness, insert material or stacking arrangement changes.

## Conclusion

I direct pressure away from the soft product, check recovery after unpacking, and verify the complete pack under representative loads.

[^1]: [ASTM D642 on compressive resistance](https://store.astm.org/standards/d642) covers testing shipping containers, components and unit loads.
[^2]: [ISTA guidelines for selecting and using test procedures](https://ista.org/docs/2020_ISTA_Guidelines.pdf) discusses choosing tests for the distribution setting.
