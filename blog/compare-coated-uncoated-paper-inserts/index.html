How do I compare paper inserts when one needs a coating?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can pay for a coated insert and still find marks on the product. The mark might come from the cut edge or the fit.

I compare paper inserts in the same geometry with the actual product. I name the problem the coating must solve, test for it under realistic handling, and compare the full cost and material evidence before I approve either option.

I use the uncoated insert as a baseline. I record where it falls short before asking a supplier to change the surface.

What problem should I ask the coating to solve?

A quote for a protective coating sounds promising. I need to tell the supplier what failed and where it happened.

I specify the failure I can observe, such as print transfer, scuffing or a moisture mark. I identify the contact area and ask the supplier which surface treatment addresses it.

I record the mark before changing the insert

I pack the product in the proposed tray or cradle and look at the points where it touches paper. I note whether the contact is on a flat face, a fold, or the exposed edge of a cut. If a mark follows a narrow line along the product, I check for a cut edge that drags during loading. If the mark sits under a broad panel, I inspect the face of the paper and any printing on it. I take pictures under the same light and keep a clean product as a reference.

I separate the condition of the insert from the condition of the product. A printed tray might lose some ink without leaving any mark on the item. A delicate product finish might show a scuff even though the insert looks untouched. I decide which result would cause rejection for each surface. I use the guide to protective inserts to consider whether changing the contact point or limiting motion might solve the issue before I add a treatment.

I also describe how the product reaches the customer. If a cold item is packed immediately after removal from storage, I ask the team to observe what happens during the normal packing period. I record the visible effect and the handling sequence. I keep any product safety or contact requirement in the specification so a change in appearance never becomes the only approval criterion.

How do I compare two samples on the same basis?

I can learn little from two samples if their board and shape differ as much as their finish. I ask for a controlled comparison.

I compare matching inserts with and without the coating when the supplier can provide them. I record any unavoidable differences and pack the same product in both samples.

I use paired samples and keep their IDs visible

I request the same board grade, cutting pattern and fold layout for both samples. I ask the supplier to identify the coated side. If the coated sample requires different stock, I ask for its grade and thickness so I can see which variables changed. I check the finished insert dimensions before testing. A tighter part can change contact pressure even when the surface looks better.

I load the product in the same direction and remove it using the intended opening sequence. I inspect both surfaces after each step. For print abrasion, ASTM describes a Sutherland rub practice for printed materials.1 If I need a controlled print test, I ask a lab what method suits the sample. I still check the assembled pack because contact at a cut edge or a fold can behave differently from a flat printed specimen.

I keep the coated and uncoated samples marked through inspection. I use the packaging sample types guide to decide when to ask for production-representative samples. I avoid approving the coating on a sample assembled by a different process from the one in the quote.

What does the coating change besides the contact surface?

A better looking flat sample can crack at a fold or create work for the packing team. I review the complete formed insert.

I check folding, fit, assembly and removal after the coating is applied. I ask the converter how the coating is produced and whether it changes any other part of the specification.

I look at folds and contact points after assembly

I form both samples and place them into the same carton. I watch for surface cracking around a tight fold, lifting at an edge, or extra effort needed to seat the insert. I look at any area that touches glue or a locking tab. If one sample sits higher in the carton, I check whether the difference came from the board thickness, fold shape or assembly method. I measure the final position before I decide that the change is acceptable.

I ask which side receives the treatment and whether it covers the whole sheet or selected areas. I confirm whether the quote includes any extra step, drying time or different tooling. If the supplier suggests leaving a glue area untreated, I request a drawing or written note that shows the boundary. I record which condition the production sample represents so the team can compare it with the order.

I use the guide to coatings on kraft packaging to frame the questions about the complete construction. The 4evergreen design guidance addresses coatings and treatments in relation to paper-based packaging recycling processes.2 I review evidence for the actual insert and its intended market before updating a disposal direction. I keep that check separate from the trial showing whether the product surface stayed clean.

When is the added coating worth buying?

A coating can pass the appearance check and still add cost without solving the problem seen on real orders.

I choose the coating when it meets a defined requirement under the intended conditions and the result justifies its cost. I record the approved material, contact side and sample before placing the order.

I compare the cost against the observed result

I ask for unit pricing at the same quantity and delivery terms. I include any additional setup, a higher minimum, and lead time. I also check whether the uncoated option could meet the requirement with a change in fit. A shaped support that keeps an item away from a rough edge can be easier to buy and pack than a coating that makes the edge less visible. I compare both choices using the same acceptance criteria.

I write the decision around an observable outcome. For example, I might require the product's display surface to remain visibly free of rub marks after loading, removal and the agreed handling trial. I define the viewing conditions and the surfaces that matter. If both samples meet the requirement, I compare their supply terms and assembly steps. If neither meets it, I ask for a new contact design and retest the finished pack.

I retain one approved sample and a short inspection record. I specify the board grade, coating identification, treated side and drawing revision on the order. I ask the supplier to tell me if any of those details change. At receipt I compare the new inserts with the sample and look at the same contact area first. That gives me a practical check for later orders.

Conclusion

I buy a coated insert when a matched trial shows it solves the defined problem and the finished construction meets my packing and purchasing requirements.
