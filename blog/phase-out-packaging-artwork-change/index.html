Sourcing & cost

How should I plan a phase-out when my packaging artwork changes?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can approve a new carton and still ship the old version if the warehouse cannot tell which stock should go first.

I decide where the old artwork can still be used, count stock by revision, and set a cutover for each affected item. I give the supplier and warehouse the same approval, hold and release instructions.

I treat a change in artwork as a change to the packing plan. I decide what each version can be used for before choosing a transition date.

Can I use the old artwork until stock runs out?

A refresh may leave the product information intact. A correction to the product information may require a much firmer boundary.

I ask the team responsible for the product and sales market to approve continued use. I record which items, markets and dates the old version can cover and hold stock that needs a decision.

I separate design changes from information changes

I compare the two versions and write down what changed. I ask whether the change affects a claim, ingredient statement, quantity, product identifier or customer requirement. For a purely visual refresh, I ask the sales team whether customers can receive both looks during the transition. For changed product information, I ask the responsible reviewer which product and artwork combinations can be used. I keep the answer by item and market because the rule for one customer or channel may differ from another.

I also check the product identifier. GS1's GTIN Management Standard provides criteria for deciding when changes to trade items require a new identifier.1 I review the change against the current requirements for the product and its distribution. I also check the customer's system before I assume the old code is still acceptable. A move to a new item identifier can affect orders and receiving even when the cartons fit in the same space.

I use the print proof approval record guide to record the decision with the two artwork versions. If a combination has not been cleared, I put it on hold. I give the warehouse a usable instruction that names the version and the affected stock. A general message about a design refresh leaves too much for the packer to interpret.

How do I count the stock that could be affected?

The stock on my shelf can be only part of the old version. I check finished goods, work in progress, and material the supplier has already printed.

I count the old version at each location and record its condition and permitted use. I include inbound orders and finished goods so I can see where the old artwork might still reach a customer.

I distinguish available, committed and held stock

Stock location What I ask the team to identify
Supplier Printed material, work in progress and finished cartons
Warehouse Available cartons, partial cases and stock on hold
Packing line Open bundles and material staged for a job
Finished goods Packed units already carrying the old artwork

I use a separate line for each item and revision. If the warehouse system has one item number for both versions, I ask for a physical check of the case labels and enough open bundles to resolve any uncertainty. I record what is already allocated. For example, if 700 old cartons remain and 250 are committed to orders allowed to use them, I work from the 450 that can still be assigned. I ask the supplier whether it can stop any printing already scheduled and what quantities have passed that point.

I use the packaging quote checklist to confirm what the supplier is committed to deliver and who decides what happens to unfinished stock. I also record any old cartons that cannot be reused. I keep those pieces separate from the usable quantity and ask the warehouse to record their final disposition. That keeps a count of physical stock from turning into an incorrect count of stock available for orders.

How do I choose a cutover date or order?

A calendar date can look clear until part of a job is already packed. I choose a boundary the line and warehouse can observe.

I use a named production order, lot, or agreed date as the change point. I confirm that the new cartons are approved and available before the first job that requires them.

I tie the transition to an identifiable batch

I decide whether the change can happen when old stock runs out or must happen at a fixed point. If both versions may ship, I can finish one lot with the old carton and begin the next with the new version. If a retailer expects the new design at launch, I identify its first affected order and reserve the approved cartons for it. I check that the supplier's schedule leaves time to receive and inspect the new stock. I do not count a factory dispatch date as the day the cartons will be ready to use.

Oracle's engineering bill of material guidance describes effective dates for component changes.2 I apply the same idea in whatever system the packing team uses. I record the last job allowed to use the old artwork and the first job that needs the new artwork. If some orders follow a different rule, I state the exception in the same record.

I also decide what happens if the new stock is late. I identify which jobs can move and which can still use an approved earlier version. I tell the team who can authorize any change. A packer should be able to follow the release instructions without deciding whether the old artwork is acceptable.

How do I prevent the two versions from getting mixed?

A case can be labeled correctly and still be opened beside an older case at the line. I check the changeover where the cartons are handled.

I label and separate revisions, clear old material before the first new job, and verify the first finished pack. I reconcile the remaining stock after the switch.

I check the warehouse and the line together

I ask the supplier to show the artwork revision on its case labels and shipment record. I give the warehouse a photo or description that makes the main difference visible. If the versions look similar, I identify a reliable detail or a code to compare. I use the guide to sample approval across multiple SKUs to keep the reference for each version tied to the right item.

Before the new job starts, I ask the team to remove any loose cartons, samples and open cases from the previous job. I check the material at the station against the new production instruction. I inspect the first finished pack and record the version used. If I see a mixed case or an unclear label, I hold the affected material and trace where it came from before resuming the job.

After the switch, I count the old stock again. I reconcile what was used, what remains and what was returned or disposed of. I close old supplier orders or update their instructions so a later delivery does not put the old version back into circulation. I retain the cutover record for customer questions and future artwork changes.

Conclusion

I approve where old artwork can still ship, count it by revision, and verify the change on the first pack after cutover.
