How do I qualify a switch from foam to folded paper protection?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can replace a foam insert and find that the new paper support holds the product in place until its first impact.

I qualify folded paper protection by matching it to the jobs the foam performs, then testing both complete packs under comparable conditions. I check product damage, assembly, variation and total cost before releasing the change.

I start with the current insert and the real product. The comparison is more useful when I can explain which feature protects each vulnerable part.

Which jobs must the folded paper replace?

A foam insert can locate the product, cushion it and protect its finish. A paper part that copies the outline may handle only one of those jobs.

I map the contact points, clearance and movement of the existing pack. I define which parts of the product can carry load and which must stay clear of the insert.

I examine the current pack before drawing a replacement

I remove the foam and note what it does at each end of the product. I check whether the product rests on a broad face, a corner or a recessed area. I look for pads that keep the product away from the outer carton and features that prevent rotation. Then I compare the visible contact points with the places the product team says are safe to support. A painted edge or a delicate control may need room even when the body can carry the load.

I look at how the foam behaves after packing. It may compress to accommodate variation in product size. If the product moves sideways before it reaches a support, I check how far it can travel. I mark the positions on a photo and use the guide to fragile product inserts to structure the comparison. I also describe what happens when the box is opened and the product is removed. A replacement that requires pulling on a fragile feature can create a new problem at unpacking.

I write a short list of requirements rather than a drawing that assumes the same shape must work. For instance, I might require clearance above a switch and limit movement at the base. I send that list to the converter with the product and the original packed sample.

How do I build a fair comparison between the two packs?

A handmade paper mockup can make a promising design look weak or give it an advantage it will not have in production.

I compare representative inserts with the same product, outer carton and packing method where possible. I record each unavoidable difference and ask which features still need a production trial.

I document the parts and the sequence

Comparison point What I check
Outer carton Same version or a documented design change
Product Same condition, dimensions and orientation
Insert Material, drawing, folds and load-bearing surfaces
Packing Placement, closure and any extra step

I ask for samples that use the intended paper grade and fold design. I check the thickness where layers overlap and measure the usable space in the formed carton. I use the inside and outside dimensions guide to communicate the clearance. If a paper support springs open or needs a tab to stay seated, I test it after the packer releases it. A sample held together by hand tells me little about how it will behave during a normal shift.

I ask someone familiar with the work to assemble both versions. I note which folds take time and where a part can be installed incorrectly. I repeat the trial with another person and compare the finished results. I record every change from the approved packing method. If the new support needs a larger carton, I include that carton in the trial instead of testing an insert in one box and pricing it in another.

I check the product and insert after ordinary removal. If the paper leaves a mark during this first bench trial, I fix the loading or contact issue before asking a lab to investigate transit performance.

What should I test before approving the switch?

A short shake can show a loose product. It cannot reproduce the range of handling conditions the parcel will see on its route.

I compare the original and revised packs using agreed product and package acceptance criteria. I ask the test provider which samples and conditions represent the planned distribution.

I choose observations that can distinguish the two designs

I write the inspection criteria before testing. I identify any damage that affects product use and any visible marks that make it unsuitable for sale. I check the insert for movement or loss of support. If a fold has crushed, I look at whether it protected the product as intended or allowed the product to touch another surface. I photograph the position of the parts before removing them so I can trace the result.

ISTA explains that a change to a packaged product can be a reason to review whether it needs retesting.1 I show the provider what changed and how the goods will travel. I ask which conditions and samples it needs to evaluate the new pack. I separate the evidence for a single item from the evidence for a bundle if the supports behave differently. I use the warehouse damage inspection guide to record findings in the same order for the control and trial samples.

I also check storage and handling before shipment. If the pack sits under load or can get damp, I ask whether those conditions should be included. I note the state of the insert when it is removed and whether the product stays centered. That gives the converter a specific feature to change if the new pack fails.

What do I record when the paper version passes?

A passing sample can be hard to reproduce if the approval names only the material family. I keep the exact construction and conditions.

I retain the approved insert, drawing, material grade and packing steps. I compare the full cost and review any material claim against the finished construction before releasing production.

I make the result useful to purchasing and receiving

I ask the converter to confirm the grade, fold sequence and any adhesive or coating. I record which side touches the product and which carton contains it. I keep a finished example and a photo of the insert before loading. At receipt I check that the new parts match those references and can be formed in the same way. If the supplier changes a fold or uses another stock, I review the affected features before releasing the delivery.

I compare the cost per usable pack. I include the insert price, freight, storage, assembly and waste from pieces the team cannot use. A flat insert may be easier to store while requiring more time at the station. I compare both results at the order quantity I expect and record any setup cost separately.

I also check the wording about the material. The 4evergreen guideline discusses construction details relevant to paper-based packaging assessment.2 I review the actual finished pack and the intended market before approving a disposal statement. I keep that evidence separate from the test that shows the insert protects the product. If the next order changes the coating or adds another part, I can check both decisions against the original record.

Conclusion

I qualify folded paper by proving that the complete pack protects the product, can be assembled consistently, and meets the agreed purchasing requirements.
