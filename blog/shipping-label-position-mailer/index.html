How do I decide where to put shipping labels on a mailer?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can leave space for a shipping label on a drawing and find it crosses a seam when the mailer is closed.

I choose a flat face on the closed, filled mailer and keep the complete carrier label clear of seams, edges and tear strips. I check the carrier's placement rules and scan a finished parcel before approving the packing instruction.

I use the real printed label and the real parcel. Together they show how much flat space the packer has and what can interfere with the scan.

Which face should carry the shipping label?

The largest panel in the artwork can become two smaller surfaces once the flaps close. I choose the face on the packed mailer.

I start with a large, flat face allowed by the carrier. I check that the address and barcodes fit entirely on that face when the parcel is filled and sealed.

I measure usable space after closing the parcel

I load the mailer with the normal product and protective parts. I close it with the intended tape or self-seal strip. I measure the uninterrupted area and compare it with the actual label size. I look at the full label, including the address block and the space around each barcode. If the lid bows, I check whether a different permitted face stays flatter. I use the guide to internal and external box dimensions to explain why the inside size tells me little about label space.

I check the carrier's instructions for the chosen service. FedEx provides guidance on creating and applying shipping labels.1 I compare that guidance with the way my packing team handles the parcel. If a label needs to sit on the top face for that workflow, I mark which side counts as the top in the packing instruction. I use a photograph of the loaded carton so the reference is easy to understand.

I check every size that will use the same label. A smaller mailer may have the same closure design with less uninterrupted space. If the standard label does not fit, I confirm whether the carrier supports another format or whether the order needs another box. I keep those cases out of the general instruction until I have a working method.

How do I avoid seams, folds and tear strips?

A label can stick well and still obstruct opening. I check what moves when the customer opens the package.

I keep the label on one continuous surface and away from sealing seams, corners and opening features. I close and open a sample to check that neither the label nor the mailer depends on the other.

I map the areas the label must avoid

Feature What I check
Closing seam The label stays on one continuous face
Flap or corner The address and barcodes remain fully visible
Tear strip The customer can reach and pull it
Return flap The label does not obstruct the return closure

I put a label on a sample after the packer closes it. I look for places where the label bridges a step in the board or crosses an edge that can flex. I pull the opening strip using the normal sequence. If the label resists the pull or starts to tear early, I move the label and try again. I check any return feature too. A customer may need a second adhesive strip after removing the first closure.

I also look at the sealing tape. If the normal taping path covers the intended label area, I check whether the layout gives the packer enough room to apply both. I do not ask operators to change a required sealing pattern just to fit an address label. I use the mailer and shipping box guide when the current construction does not leave a workable face. I mark the approved area on a formed sample before asking the converter to reserve it in the artwork.

What should I check about print and scan quality?

A label can fit on the correct face and still wrinkle, fade or sit beside an old barcode. I check the complete surface.

I print the carrier label at its supported size, apply it smoothly, and verify it using the normal scan workflow. I remove or cover obsolete shipping marks according to the applicable instructions.

I check the label as the warehouse uses it

I print through the software and printer the team will use during dispatch. I verify that the printer has not clipped the label or changed its scale. I check contrast, missing lines and any smudge across the address or barcode. I apply the label to a clean, suitable area and look for creases or bubbles. If the pack already carries another shipping label, I check the carrier's guidance before reusing it. USPS's package preparation page includes guidance on address placement and preparing packages.2

I scan the label with the normal device and check that the order record matches the parcel. I repeat the check on a few representative boxes. If one scan fails only on a bulging lid, I look at placement and surface shape before changing the printer. If several labels show the same missing lines, I check the printer and supplies. I record which observation led to the correction.

I check that the label is still readable when the package reaches the dispatch area. If a label edge has lifted or a face has rubbed against another package, I examine the contact point. I make any change with the carrier's instructions and warehouse process in mind, then repeat the same scan.

How do I write a placement instruction the team can repeat?

A note saying put label on top can be unclear on a mailer with printing on both sides. I show the exact face and boundaries.

I give the packer a photo of the closed parcel with the approved label zone and visible landmarks. I verify the first packed order and define what to do when a label or box falls outside the instruction.

I show the result next to the packing steps

I take a photo of the closed parcel from the angle the packer sees. I show where the label belongs relative to the hinge and opening strip. I include a view of the finished parcel with the label in place. If the same mailer can be packed in two orientations, I check that the photo still gives the correct instruction or add a separate view. I keep personal addresses and order data out of training images by using a clearly marked sample label.

I specify which label format and carton sizes the instruction covers. I ask someone unfamiliar with the layout to pack a test order and apply the label without coaching. I look for places where the wording could lead them to put it across a seam. I also show when to stop and ask for another box or label format. I use the warehouse damage inspection guide to record placement problems found after delivery.

I retain the approved photo and packing revision. If the carrier label size, mailer shape or closure changes, I repeat the fit and scan checks. I update the instruction after the revised arrangement passes. This lets the next shift use the same reference without guessing where the label should sit.

Conclusion

I place the full label on a flat face, clear the opening features, and verify the finished parcel with the normal scan process.
