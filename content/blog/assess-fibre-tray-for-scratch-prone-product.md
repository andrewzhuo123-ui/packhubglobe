# How should I assess a fibre tray when the product surface scratches?

I can replace a tray and still see the same scratch if the product drags against the opening every time someone lifts it out.

**I inspect the product before loading, after loading and after handling. I match each mark to the closest contact point and compare changes to the tray shape, finish or support using the same product.**

I look for the first stage at which a mark appears. That tells me whether to adjust the packing sequence or investigate movement in the closed box.

## How do I find out when the scratch appears?

A scratch discovered during unpacking can come from loading, transport or removal. I set up samples that let me distinguish the stages.

**I use separate samples for loading, handling and removal checks. I photograph the same surface before each trial and record when a new mark first becomes visible.**

### I inspect a clean reference first

I select products with the intended finish and look for existing marks under consistent lighting. I identify a feature that helps me locate the surface again, such as a seam or a control. I take a photograph before packing and keep a clean item for comparison. If the product carries a removable protective film during assembly, I record when it comes off. That step can change which surface touches the tray.

I load the first product slowly and watch its path past the rim. I check whether a corner touches the tray before it reaches the seated position. I inspect a second sample after closing and ordinary handling. I remove another sample using the instructions a customer will receive. If the mark only appears after removal, I look at where the user grips the item and whether it tilts into an edge. I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to preserve the positions before moving the contents.

I also check whether the mark is loose fibre, material transfer, or a cut in the product finish. I ask the product team how to inspect it without changing the evidence. I record the original appearance before wiping or cleaning a sample. If the mark changes with viewing angle, I document the angle as well as the position.

## Which features of the tray should I compare?

A tray can feel smooth on its main face and still have a raised edge that reaches the product. I inspect the specific areas that touch.

**I compare the rim, trimmed edges, ribs and support points. I ask the converter how those areas are formed and request representative samples when the finish or geometry changes.**

### I map contact instead of judging the whole tray by touch

| Area I inspect | What I ask the converter to verify |
| --- | --- |
| Entry rim | Whether the product can pass without scraping |
| Trimmed edge | Whether the finish is consistent along the contact path |
| Support rib | Where it touches and how much movement it permits |
| Recess base | Whether the product remains seated after closing |

I compare a tray from the trial with one made using the intended production process. I look for a local ridge or rough spot where the product touched. I check whether the mark follows that feature across several samples. I also inspect the matching product area. A rounded edge may still leave a mark if the recess is too tight or if the product moves against it repeatedly. I use the [guide to protective inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) to frame the fit and support questions for the converter.

Huhtamaki describes smooth molded fibre as one option within its fibre technology portfolio.[^1] I ask my converter which surface options it can make on the tray I need. I also ask whether the smoother surface applies to the inside, outside or both. I compare the trimmed area and folds separately if they are made or finished differently. A general description of the material gives me less information than a representative sample with the contact points marked.

## How do I compare a fit change with a surface change?

A smoother tray can reduce a mark while leaving the same movement. A looser tray can make loading easier and give the product more room to rub.

**I compare one change at a time when possible. I check loading clearance, restraint and visible marks on the same product finish before deciding which change solves the problem.**

### I choose a trial around the observed contact

If the scratch begins at the entry rim, I compare a revised entry shape with the current tray. I keep the seated supports the same so I can see whether the new opening fixes the loading step. If the mark appears only after motion, I check whether the product can rock or slide. I try a support at a less sensitive area and see whether it stops the motion. If the contact is necessary, I ask the converter for a different surface in that area or review a separate protective piece.

I keep the carton, product and closure consistent when comparing the options. I record any change that cannot be avoided. I use the [inside and outside box dimensions guide](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) to communicate where the available clearance changes after the pack is formed. I also check how the revised tray affects opening. A tight grip may stop movement and require the customer to drag the product across the rim to remove it.

If I add a sleeve or liner, I check its thickness and whether it stays in place. I ask how it changes assembly and material documentation. I include it in the final trial rather than assuming the earlier tray results still apply.

## What should I verify before releasing the revised tray?

A single clean sample can hide a change in the next batch. I keep the approval tied to the actual tray and its intended packing method.

**I check representative trays with the product, agree on the visual limit, and test the complete pack when the route requires it. I retain the accepted tray and assembly record.**

### I make the comparison reproducible at receipt

I identify the surfaces that must remain free of marks and the light and angle used to inspect them. I show the reviewer an accepted product beside the tested sample. I check whether the tray from each representative sample seats the same way. If there is a difference, I note where it occurs and ask the converter whether it reflects a change in the tool, trimming or material. I keep the tray identity with the results so a later batch can be compared.

I use a handling plan that fits the complete package and the intended route. ISTA's design and testing guidance discusses preparing a packaged product and selecting a test for distribution conditions.[^2] I ask the lab or quality team which checks can reveal the movement I am concerned about. I photograph the tray and product before I move them after the trial. I compare the location of any new mark with the earlier samples.

I record the approved tray revision, product orientation, and any additional support or protective piece. I tell the warehouse which defects require a hold. If a finish, tool or product shape changes later, I repeat the affected inspection using the same reference.

## Conclusion

I find when the scratch appears, change the contact that causes it, and verify the finished tray with the real product and shipping pack.
