How do I approve packaging samples for multiple SKUs?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can approve a sample for one product and miss a cap, insert or label change on another. A shared box drawing does not settle every variant.

I approve packaging samples for multiple SKUs with a matrix that links each variant to its drawing, contents, artwork and evidence. I group only variants with the same relevant risks and keep unresolved variants on hold.

I build the matrix before ordering samples. This helps me explain where one sample can support several decisions and where I need a separate check.

Which variant differences belong in my sample matrix?

A SKU list may show only a product name and colour. I need to see what changes inside and outside the pack.

I record product dimensions and mass, cap or protrusion geometry, insert and box revisions, artwork revision, intended packing method and the owner of each check. I identify unknowns and resolve them before granting approval.

I build one row per saleable variant

I start with what a packer has to handle. For an illustrative range with two bottles, the short bottle may use the same carton as the tall one but a different insert. A limited edition may share both parts while using unique artwork and a distinct retail code. I create a row for each, even when I expect to reuse evidence. The rows show which shared component carries the same drawing and which part changes. I also add a column for product condition, such as filled bottle with production cap, because an empty demonstration bottle will answer fewer questions.

AM-120-GIFT C07 I03 A13 Gift artwork and retail code

I confirm the baseline with the supplier

I ask for the revision on each drawing and whether the sample uses the intended materials. I compare this with the packaging quote checklist so that component or finish differences do not disappear between enquiry and sample order. I identify any difference that a supplier cannot represent on this round. If a sample was cut by hand, I note whether that affects confidence in folds, edge finish or assembly. The matrix records exactly what the sample can support, then I ask for the missing evidence on the next appropriate version.

When can one sample cover more than one variant?

Ordering every combination can consume time and money. I first work out which packaging risks the shared sample actually represents.

I share evidence only when the relevant construction and use are the same. I check the tightest and loosest credible fits, heavier or awkward variants, and unique artwork separately wherever those differences affect approval.

I choose a representative sample for each risk

I might use one carton sample to check whether a common locking tab closes and remains flush. I can share that observation across rows with the same board, drawing and closure. To check the product fit, I look for differences in geometry, cap movement and how far the product sits into the insert. The tallest item may define closure clearance, while a shorter item may move more in the same box. I select a sample for each failure mode I need to assess. If no one can tell me which variant creates the risk, I request the actual variants and compare them before deciding.

I record the grouping decision

The matrix might say “C07 closure shared by AM-100 and AM-120, fit reviewed separately using I02 and I03.” This wording explains why the rows share one piece of evidence and where the decision stops. The structural and printed sample comparison helps me choose the physical or print stage for each check. I use a first pass to expose missing evidence before I schedule the final review.

When I need distribution evidence, I describe the exact product and pack construction to the test provider. FedEx's package testing instructions describe supplying a packaged article for testing and inspecting the product after the procedure.1 I ask whether the configuration I propose is representative and put the answer in the matrix. A fit observation in the office does not provide the same evidence as route testing.

How do I make physical and print checks traceable?

A sample can pass during a meeting and lose its identity once it is put back on the shelf. I label it and capture how it was checked.

I assign each sample an ID and record the SKU, contents, sample revision, reviewer, method and result. I photograph the assembled pack and link any separate artwork or barcode evidence to the corresponding SKU row.

I make the sample easy to reproduce

I write the sample ID on a removable tag and take a photo that includes it. I record the orientation, insert position and how the pack was closed. I measure relevant clearances at agreed points and note the tool used. If a fit changes when the product is turned or capped, I show which orientation was approved. I also record which person did the work and whether the packer needed an unusual fold or adjustment. In a later review, I want the team to recreate the method instead of interpreting a single photo.

I match printed identity to the finished product

For each printed variant I confirm the name, artwork and intended code against the SKU record. I check whether the expected finish, label position and artwork can be evaluated on the current sample. I leave the row open for finish approval if the sample has no equivalent finish. GS1's guidance identifies symbol position and the scanning environment as considerations in assessing barcode quality.2 If the sales channel requires formal verification, I capture the applicable report with the sample ID. I avoid using a scan from one variant to approve codes on several other printed variants.

I list the acceptable result before checking. For example, I can record that the product must release without catching on the flap and that the cap must remain clear during closure. If a criterion relies on a tolerance, I ask the buyer and supplier to agree the value and measurement method before I judge the sample.

How do I release only the variants that passed?

Approving a shared component can accidentally sound like permission to manufacture every artwork version. I make the decision at the SKU level.

I mark each SKU as approved, held or awaiting new evidence. I identify the exact files and samples released, tell the supplier what may proceed, and retain the acknowledgment beside the matrix.

I tie the scope to the purchase order

I prepare a short release list with each SKU, carton and insert revision, artwork file and approved sample ID. For the example range, I may release AM-100 and AM-120 while holding AM-120-GIFT for a new proof. I name that hold even if the gift version uses the same components. If the purchase order is already drafted, I compare its lines with the release list and ask the supplier to acknowledge the quantities and revisions they plan to run. I use the sample types guide to specify the evidence required on a held row and avoid requests for an unspecified “better sample.”

I close the loop when something changes

If a revised cap arrives, I start with every row that depends on that cap and check what the change affects. It may require a fit review and no change to the artwork. A new illustration may need a proof without another insert sample if the production details are unchanged. I record the reasoning and assign the check before adjusting any release status. For later orders I compare the current purchase order and supplier files with the retained approved set.

I keep the previous matrix revision, its evidence and the approval message together. I issue a new dated matrix when a decision changes. A packer or supplier can then find which parts to assemble and the buyer can see why a related variant is still on hold.

Conclusion

I use one row per SKU and share only evidence that represents the same risk. Each release names the sample, files and variant it covers.
