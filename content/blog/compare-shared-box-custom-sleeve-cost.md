# How do I compare the cost of a shared box with a custom sleeve?

I can save on printed boxes and lose the saving if every sleeve needs hand fitting or one design runs out early.

**I compare a common box and variant sleeves with dedicated printed boxes at the same finished quantity. I include setup, sleeve assembly, freight and the stock I may be unable to use.**

I first check whether the common box works for every item. Then I compare the cost of building and maintaining each option.

## Which products can use the same box?

A sleeve can change the appearance of a box. It cannot make a loose product fit or create room for a tall closure.

**I share the box only across products that meet the same fit, protection and presentation requirements. I test the smallest and largest relevant loads before pricing the shared structure.**

### I separate product fit from the artwork choice

I lay out each product with the parts that go inside the box. I check which items need a different tray or pad. If a smaller item moves, I ask whether a simple insert can hold it in place. I include that insert in the shared option's cost and check whether it adds a packing step. If a larger item pushes against the lid, I test a different structure for that item. I avoid sizing the whole family around one unusual product unless the larger box also works for the rest.

I use the [inside and outside box dimensions guide](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) to specify two different measurements. I check the space inside for the product and insert. Then I check the outside of the loaded and closed box for the sleeve. If the lid bows or the box wraps overlap at one end, I measure the area the sleeve must pass over. I review that part on a physical sample rather than choosing the sleeve from the nominal box size.

I also check the printed information each variant needs. If some information has to stay with the box after the sleeve is removed, I plan where it will appear. I ask the brand and quality teams to review the intended use before I assume the sleeve can carry all variant-specific information.

## How do I put both quotes on the same cost basis?

One quote can list a low price for the common box and omit the sleeve tooling or assembly. I compare the complete pack.

**I ask for the same usable quantities and delivery terms. I include the base box, sleeves, variant inserts, tooling, packing labor and freight that apply to each option.**

### I build a comparison by variant

| Quote line | Common box with sleeves | Dedicated printed boxes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Box units | Shared quantity plus any reserve | Quantity by artwork variant |
| Print and setup | Sleeve artwork and setup per quote | Box artwork and setup per quote |
| Assembly | Fit sleeve and check orientation | Check any assembly still required |
| Stock risk | Shared boxes and separate sleeve counts | Finished boxes tied to each variant |

I ask the supplier which minimum applies to each sleeve design. Esko's Phoenix documentation describes grouping print jobs by factors such as substrate and planning sheet layouts around quantities.[^1] I use that to ask whether the converter can quote several sleeve designs together and still produce the quantities I need. I keep that request separate from the quotation for the common box. A shared box run does not guarantee a shared sleeve run.

I measure the work to put the sleeve on. I include the time to orient it, slide it into place and verify the correct design. If a sleeve needs a tab or adhesive, I include that material and check whether the station needs extra space. I compare the [landed cost](/blog/compare-packaging-quotes-landed-cost/) on the same shipment assumptions before adding the work the warehouse must do. If the supplier offers pre-sleeved packs, I check whether that changes the freight, storage or flexibility before comparing it with sleeves supplied separately.

## When does the common box reduce my inventory risk?

I can reuse a blank box across several products. A shortage of one sleeve can still stop the corresponding orders.

**I compare the number of finished packs each option supports in slow and fast scenarios. I count common boxes once and check each variant's sleeve and insert stock separately.**

### I test what happens when the mix changes

Suppose I plan to sell 600 units of one item and 400 of another. With 1,000 common boxes, 600 sleeves for the first item and 400 for the second, I can make the intended mix. If sales move to 700 and 300, the boxes still cover total demand. I still need another 100 sleeves for the first item. I ask how quickly I can obtain them and whether that order would meet the supplier's minimum. With dedicated boxes, I would need another 100 complete printed boxes in that scenario.

Oracle's bill of material documentation describes linking components and quantities to an assembly.[^2] I write a short component list for each variant so I can see which items limit output. If both variants need the same insert, I count its stock across both. If they need different inserts, I check the available quantity separately. I compare the cost and time to cover a shortfall, not just the value of the extra box stock.

I also consider unused sleeves after an artwork change. A sleeve may cost less than a finished printed box, but it can still be tied to a dated campaign or discontinued product. I record which parts can be used elsewhere and include any expected write-off in the comparison.

## What should I verify before approving the shared design?

A sleeve that fits one sample can catch on a packed box or slide out of place after handling. I test it before ordering the full run.

**I check fit, appearance and handling on representative closed boxes. I verify the correct sleeve is easy to select, then record the conditions that make the shared option worthwhile.**

### I check the result at the packing station

I give the team the boxes and sleeves as they will arrive. I ask it to follow the proposed assembly sequence. I look for torn edges, scuffs and sleeves that are too loose. I check whether each sleeve sits in the intended position when the pack is picked up and opened. If the operator needs to squeeze the box or use two attempts, I record the time and ask the converter to review the fit.

I verify the variant identity before the finished pack leaves the station. If two sleeve designs are easy to confuse, I add a clear item reference to the supply label and the packing instruction. I use the [guide to approving samples across multiple SKUs](/blog/packaging-sample-approval-multiple-skus/) to keep the sample record tied to the correct variant. I confirm who checks the final combination and what happens if one sleeve is missing.

I compare the revised estimate with the dedicated box quote after the trial. I include the cost of any adjustment and keep the demand assumptions beside the decision. If the range grows or the assembly time rises, I can rerun the comparison. I retain the accepted box, sleeve and fit record together so a later supplier change can be checked against the same result.

## Conclusion

I choose a shared box when the finished packs fit, the sleeves can be supplied on time, and the full cost and inventory exposure justify it.

[^1]: [Esko Phoenix planning and imposition](https://www.esko.com/en/products/phoenix) describes grouping print jobs and planning layouts.
[^2]: [Oracle guidance on bill of material entry](https://docs.oracle.com/en/applications/jd-edwards/supply-chain-manufacturing/9.2/eoapd/understanding-bill-of-material-entry.html) explains recording the components required for an assembly.
