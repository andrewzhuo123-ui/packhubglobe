How do I compare supplier lead-time estimates on the same basis?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can pick the supplier quoting fifteen days and learn later that its clock starts after a proof and ends before collection.

I compare supplier lead-time estimates from the same starting conditions to the date I can use the packaging. I separate proof approval, material readiness, production, transport and receiving, and check the calendar and quantities behind each milestone.

I ask each supplier to convert its estimate into dates for the same job. Then I compare the events that could move those dates.

What starts the supplier's clock?

A quote can say twenty days from order and leave room for different interpretations. I ask what must happen before day one.

I confirm whether the clock starts after order acceptance, deposit, artwork approval, material arrival or another event. I put an owner and expected date beside each prerequisite.

I list the conditions each supplier needs

I start with the earliest date I can issue a complete request. I check whether the structure and artwork are already approved. If a new proof is required, I ask when the supplier can send it and how much time my team needs to review it. I ask whether the supplier can reserve stock or a production slot during that review. Some steps can overlap. I record the actual dependency instead of adding every duration together.

Start condition What I ask for
Purchase order Date the supplier can accept the order
Deposit Date it expects payment to be confirmed
Artwork or sample Approval needed before material or production release
Material and tooling Availability and any work before production

Oracle's lead-time guidance distinguishes activities before, during and after processing.1 I use that separation to make the quote readable. I use the packaging quote checklist to confirm which materials, samples and tools the estimate assumes. If the supplier says it has stock available, I ask whether the stock is allocated to my order or simply expected to be available. I also ask who can confirm the start date. I record the time zone and working calendar when teams in different locations exchange approvals.

Where does the estimate end?

Ready to ship and ready to use can be several handoffs apart. I ask for both dates before comparing suppliers.

I identify when the supplier releases the packed goods and when approved stock reaches the packing team. I include collection, transit, delivery booking and receiving work between those points.

I check the arrival date at the actual site

I ask when the factory will finish its inspection and prepare the shipment. If the buyer arranges collection, I ask when a carrier can pick up the cases and what booking details it needs. If the supplier arranges delivery, I confirm the address, service and date in its quote. I compare the same destination across both offers. A quote to a port or transfer warehouse cannot stand in for a delivery date at the packing site.

I keep transport estimates separate from factory commitments. FedEx's shipping estimate guidance explains how shipment details and service choices affect the quote.2 I use the applicable service and actual shipment details to check the estimate. I ask whether the date depends on collection by a particular cutoff. I also record any additional handling or transfer after delivery. I use the landed cost comparison guide to align delivery terms when the suppliers quote different scopes.

At the warehouse, I confirm when the team can receive and inspect the goods. If the material must be moved to the line or sampled before use, I include that time. I ask the supplier to state whether its estimate covers the full order or only the first release. A first delivery can help if it contains enough accepted parts for the work I have scheduled.

How do I compare estimates with different assumptions?

A shorter estimate can depend on stock the supplier has not reserved or a delivery service I do not plan to buy.

I ask for the same quantity, specification, delivery scope and calendar. I compare an expected schedule and a slower case based on the main uncertainty each supplier identifies.

I ask both suppliers to show the effect of a delay

For a simple example, Supplier A quotes twelve working days from proof approval to dispatch. Supplier B quotes sixteen working days from order acceptance to delivery and includes a proof review in that period. I ask A for the proof and shipping dates before comparing the offers. I ask B how many review days it allowed and whether the estimate still works if I need a correction. I check whether both suppliers are using working days and which holidays apply.

I then identify the event that has the most effect on the promised date. A may need a special board that has not reached its plant. B may be waiting for a finishing slot. I ask what each supplier can confirm today and what remains provisional. I check whether a substitute stock or an express shipment would preserve the required date, and what approval or extra cost that option would need.

I compare the schedules using my latest acceptable date for usable material. If the expected dates both work, I look at which estimate has a more credible recovery path. I also ask whether the first release can cover the work until the rest arrives. I count pieces by item and revision so a large partial shipment cannot hide the missing component that stops the run.

How do I keep the selected schedule current after ordering?

A quote is a snapshot. I need to know when a change at the factory starts to threaten my receipt date.

I record the agreed milestones and owners on the order. I ask for confirmation when each one is reached and set a decision point for late approvals, material delays or missed collections.

I track events I can verify

I ask the supplier to confirm the production slot once the prerequisites are met. I check the proof against the agreed artwork revision and record the approval date. I use the packaging print proof guide when a proof needs a specific finish or material check. If the proof goes back for correction, I ask the supplier which later dates change and which still hold. I keep the old dates beside the updated ones so the team can see the effect.

I confirm when the shipment is ready and ask for the packed quantities and collection details. I compare them with the dates on the order. If a milestone slips, I ask for the next confirmed event and recalculate when the packaging could become usable. I identify who decides whether to wait, split the shipment or change the production sequence.

After receipt, I compare the quoted and actual dates. I record where the difference arose and whether the estimate included that step. I keep the notes with the order for the next quote. If one supplier repeatedly estimates only factory time, I ask for both factory and arrival dates from the beginning of the next comparison.

Conclusion

I compare lead times using the same start conditions and usable receipt date, then track the milestones that can change the result.
