# Which package configuration should I send to a transit test lab?

I can pay for a transit test and discover that the sample used a different insert from the parcel I intend to ship.

**I send a representative product in its complete shipping pack. I agree with the lab on the route, configurations, sample count and acceptance criteria, then document the parts and packing steps for each sample.**

I choose samples around a decision the report needs to support. That helps me identify which configurations can be considered together and which need separate evidence.

## What should the sample contain?

A carton and insert can look complete on the bench. The lab also needs the load and closure that determine how they behave.

**I include the actual product, inner packaging, supports, outer carton and normal closure. I agree on any substitute load with the lab and record what the substitution can represent.**

### I reproduce the parcel that will leave the warehouse

I pack the item using the intended sequence. I use the same product orientation, insert and closure as the production pack. I include accessories and paperwork if they change fit or movement. A folded instruction sheet can raise a product against a lid, while an omitted accessory can leave room for a tray to shift. I use the [guide to protective inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) to identify the support points I need to document.

I ask the lab how it wants to receive the samples. Some arrangements call for a separate shipping container so the sample itself reaches the lab without first going through ordinary parcel handling. FedEx provides instructions for preparing packages for its testing service.[^1] I follow the selected lab's own intake requirements. I also ask whether the lab wants spare parts, unopened samples, or instructions for assembling the pack at its site.

I record any difference from production. If the actual product cannot be provided, I ask whether an agreed substitute can represent its weight, shape, stiffness or vulnerable points. A substitute can help answer a narrow packaging question. I record the limits before using the result to approve a production pack. I ask for another test if the remaining difference affects the decision.

## How do I decide which variants need separate samples?

The largest or heaviest pack can be demanding, while a smaller load might move more. I choose variants by the way they could fail.

**I compare weight, movement, contact points and closure across the range. I agree with the lab which configurations address different risks and record which versions each result is intended to cover.**

### I compare actual combinations before I choose

| Configuration | Reason I might include it |
| --- | --- |
| Heaviest bundle | Higher load on the base and supporting parts |
| Smallest single item | More room to move in a shared carton |
| Tallest product | Less clearance under flaps or a top pad |
| Delicate finish | Greater sensitivity to scuffing at contact points |

I check these conditions on real packed samples. If the heaviest bundle uses a different box from the single item, I treat that as a distinct configuration. If two variants share every packaging part but differ in finish, I ask whether they need a separate appearance check. I identify what changes when a customer orders more than one product. A bundle that introduces a divider or a different product orientation may need its own evidence.

I also check the route. ISTA's design and testing guidance discusses selecting tests in relation to the distribution environment.[^2] I give the lab the actual shipment details instead of choosing a procedure from the carton name. I explain whether the pack travels as a single parcel, in a master case, or on a pallet. I ask the lab how many samples it needs and whether it needs different examples for separate routes. I retain its decision with the scope of the test.

## What information should I send with each sample?

Two cartons may look identical after sealing. I label the samples and give the lab enough information to tell their configurations apart.

**I attach a unique sample reference and provide the packing list, drawing revisions, product details and assembly instructions. I identify any difference from the production version.**

### I make each configuration easy to reproduce

I assign a reference to the complete packed sample. I list the outer carton, insert, closure and product by the version used. I note the orientation and the order in which the packer adds the parts. If the lab will prepare the samples, I include photographs or clear instructions for each step. I use the [packaging sample types guide](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to identify whether the carton came from the planned production process.

I measure and weigh the closed parcel. I record those readings beside the sample reference rather than using a catalog dimension. I tell the lab which product surfaces or closures are vulnerable and what condition the item was in before testing. If the pack contains several components, I identify where each item belongs. That helps the inspector distinguish a moved product from one that started in a different position.

I also record changes that happened during packing. If a spacer needed trimming or the carton closed only after adding tape, I note that on the sample sheet. I ask whether the sample should be replaced before testing. If the deviation is intentional, I make it visible in the report scope so it does not disappear when the result is shared later.

## What do I agree on before the lab opens the samples?

A package can arrive intact and still have a damaged product or a gift box that cannot be sold. I define the inspection before testing begins.

**I agree with the lab on the procedure, sample count, opening sequence and acceptance criteria. I specify which product, packaging and appearance conditions it should document.**

### I match the report to the decision I need

I name the parts that need to remain functional or visibly undamaged. I ask the product team how to inspect them after handling. I separate acceptable marks on a transport carton from unacceptable marks on the retail pack. I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to specify the photographs and observations I need before anyone removes the contents.

I agree on who will inspect the sample and who makes the release decision. Some products need a functional check that the lab cannot perform by looking at the package. I ask how the samples should be returned or retained for that check. I confirm whether the report will identify each sample and record any departure from the agreed procedure. I want to understand the result even when one sample passes and another shows a different issue.

After the report arrives, I match it to the sample records and the intended shipment. I note which configurations and routes it covers. If I change a part, I ask whether the original result still supports the new pack or whether I need another comparison. I keep the report, packing instructions and approved revision together for the warehouse and the next buyer.

## Conclusion

I send the real shipping configuration, agree on the test scope and inspection, and retain the exact packing record with the result.

[^2]: [ISTA guidance on getting started with design and testing](https://www.ista.org/getting_started_with_design.php) discusses preparing for packaged-product testing.
