Sourcing & cost

Who owns the tooling after I pay for it?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can pay a tooling charge and discover at reorder that the supplier considers the die part of its own production equipment.

I confirm packaging tooling ownership in the order terms before I pay. I record who owns each physical tool, where it is stored, who can use it, and what I can recover or transfer if the relationship changes.

I treat the invoice, the tool and the design files as separate items. I use the following checks to make sure the quote and the agreement describe the same arrangement.

What did I buy when I paid the tooling charge?

A quote can use the word tooling for several costs. A single line gives me little to check when I place the next order.

I ask whether the price buys a named physical tool, covers setup, or pays for access to a supplier-owned tool. I record each item and its agreed treatment separately.

I ask for a list tied to the pack

For a folding carton, I ask whether the charge covers the cutting and creasing die, any support tooling, or a separate tool for a finish. I ask for the identifier of each physical item and the drawing revision it serves. The packaging quote checklist helps me keep these costs separate from proofing, printing and freight. I ask for a revised quote if the supplier groups everything under one number and expects me to infer what I can use later.

BOBST describes tooling and support services for several converting operations.1 I use that distinction when I review a quote. A die used to form the blank may be a different item from a tool that applies a decorative finish. I also ask if the supplier expects a routine make-ready charge on reorders even when the same die stays in service. If the quote says that the tooling charge is waived, I check what changes in the terms.

I ask how the order identifies the items I paid for. A useful reference can be a tool number tied to the purchase order and the current drawing. I avoid treating a file name or a picture of the carton as the only record. I want enough information to trace the charge to the tool that actually made the order.

How do I define storage, reuse and disposal?

A tool may be available for my next order while it stays at the plant. I want to know how long the plant will keep it.

I record the storage location, the expected retention period, and who can approve reuse, repair or disposal. I ask how the supplier will notify me before the tool is changed or discarded.

I work through a repeat-order scenario

I imagine placing the same order after a quiet year. I ask whether the tool will still be available, what storage period applies and whether the clock starts on the first order or the last use. If the supplier stores the tool at a subcontractor, I ask who controls access. I also ask whether it can use a tool made for my pack to produce for another buyer. I record the response alongside the ownership terms so the two points can be checked independently.

Question What I record
Where is the tool? Plant, custodian and tool reference
How long will it be held? Retention period and start date
Who can approve reuse? Permitted orders and approval contact
What if it wears out? Inspection, repair and replacement procedure

I ask for a picture of the tool label or another record showing the identifier. On a repeat order I compare that reference with the approved structure. If the supplier proposes a repair, I ask whether it changes the cutting or crease positions and which sample will confirm the result. I distinguish a worn component from a request to redesign the carton.

I put a review date in the purchase record. Before that date I decide whether to request continued storage, schedule a reorder or arrange release. I ask for notice before the supplier discards the tool and record the notice period agreed for my order.

What can I transfer if I change converters?

Owning the physical die can give me a way to ask for its release. The receiving plant still needs to check whether it fits its process.

I confirm what can be released and ask the next converter to review the tool and files before transfer. I compare shipping and adaptation with the cost of rebuilding for its equipment.

I review the production setup with the receiving plant

I send the tool reference, structural drawing, photographs and any available layout information. I ask the receiving plant to check its equipment, mounting, sheet layout and related operations. BOBST's NOVACUT documentation gives one example of a machine with its own configuration and operating specifications.2 I treat the receiving plant's written assessment as the basis for a reuse decision. A successful order at the old plant cannot answer every setup question at the new one.

I list the digital items I need separately. I may need a structural CAD file, a reference PDF and the approved graphics. I ask which files the old converter can release and which will be useful to the new converter. The dieline preparation guide helps me describe the structure I need to hand over. I also ask whether the new plant needs to prepare a fresh production layout even if it can reproduce the carton shape from my drawing.

I ask for a complete transfer price. It can include packing, freight, inspection and any work needed before a trial. I compare that cost and the likely delay with making a new tool locally. I document who pays for the return and when the old supplier must make the tool available. Then I ask the receiving plant for a sample that confirms the relevant dimensions and assembly.

What should I write into the order before approving it?

A good conversation with a salesperson can be hard to reconstruct after the account changes hands. I put the important decisions in the order record.

I name each tool and record its ownership, custody, permitted use, maintenance, release conditions and associated files. I ask the supplier to confirm the terms against the quote before production.

I keep the terms beside the approved drawing

I make a short schedule with one row per tool. I identify the pack version and quote reference. I record the agreed owner and the custodian if they differ. I add the release contact, notice period and transfer charges if they apply. I record how damage, wear and replacement will be handled. If the order already has terms covering these items, I ask the buyer and supplier to resolve any mismatch before using the schedule.

I also define what happens if the carton changes. If a supplier modifies the original die, I ask whether I can still order the older version. If I need both versions, I ask for separate identifiers and confirm which sample belongs to each one. I use the packaging sample types guide to specify what to review after a tool is made, repaired or moved.

I save the accepted quote, order terms, structural revision and first approved sample record together. I update that record after a repair or transfer. At reorder I can see which tool the supplier plans to use and who approved the last change. If I hand the account to a colleague, the record gives them a place to start without reopening the original negotiations.

Conclusion

I confirm ownership, storage and release for each tool in writing, then check machine compatibility before counting on reuse at a different plant.
