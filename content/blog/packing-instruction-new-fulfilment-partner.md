# How do I prepare a packing instruction for a new fulfilment partner?

I can send a warehouse a photo of a finished parcel and still leave the packer guessing which insert belongs under the product.

**I write a packing instruction around an approved sample. I list the parts, show the assembly order and closure, and give the operator clear stop conditions. I then watch a new packer use it on representative orders.**

I build the instruction at the bench where the work happens. Each step should help the packer choose a part, place it, or check the result.

## What does the packer need before opening the first order?

A product name may be familiar to my team and mean little to a new warehouse. I identify the order and parts using labels it can see.

**I show which orders the instruction covers and list each component by its warehouse identifier, revision and quantity. I include a picture where similar parts are easy to confuse.**

### I match my component list to the station

I start with the approved packed sample and lay out its parts in order. I identify the outer carton, product, support, wrap, leaflet and closure. I give the partner the identifiers it will see on inventory labels. If the supplier code and the warehouse code differ, I put both in the handoff record and use the warehouse code at the station. I indicate which version the operator should pick when older stock remains nearby.

I check whether a rule applies to all orders or only to one combination. A single bottle may use a small carton while a bundle needs a divided mailer. I show the order condition that selects each instruction. I ask which screen or label the operator sees before packing so I can use a name the operator can recognize. I use the [guide to sample approval across multiple SKUs](/blog/packaging-sample-approval-multiple-skus/) to match the reference pack with the right item and configuration.

I also identify the parts that must be staged before a shift. The Lean Enterprise Institute describes standardized work using elements that include work sequence and standard in-process inventory.[^1] I use those concepts to check that the written sequence and the available parts support the same operation. If a part is missing, the operator needs an explicit decision instead of guessing what to use.

## How do I show the sequence without overloading the instruction?

A long list of notes can bury the single detail that keeps a closure from rubbing against the lid. I put each check beside the action it controls.

**I show the actions in order with a photo at each decision that affects fit or protection. I show what a correct result looks like before the operator moves on.**

### I show placement from the operator's viewpoint

| Step | What I show at the station |
| --- | --- |
| Form the carton | Which flap closes first and how the base sits |
| Seat the insert | Which side faces up and where it locks |
| Load the product | Orientation and visible clearance around the closure |
| Close and label | Sealing pattern and approved label face |

I take photographs from the angle the operator sees. I use a close view for an important contact point and a wider view for orientation. If a part looks almost the same upside down, I include a feature that distinguishes the two positions. I also show the wrong position if it has caused confusion during the trial. I keep the photo beside the relevant instruction so the operator does not have to search another document while holding a product.

I confirm how the package will close. FedEx's packing guidance includes cushioning, closure and labeling considerations.[^2] I check the service requirements and show the exact closure materials and label position the team should use. I also show how the completed parcel should look. If tape hides a gap I need the operator to notice, I put that check before the taping step. I keep the approved finished sample at the station during the first run.

## What should tell the operator to stop and ask?

An operator may be able to close the lid with extra force and assume the order is ready. I define the cases that need a decision.

**I write visible stop conditions for missing parts, damaged packaging, a poor fit or an incorrect closure. I name the approved substitutions and the person who can release an exception.**

### I make the exception as clear as the normal step

I use a short description of what the operator can see. If a lid rises after the lock is engaged, I tell the operator to hold the parcel and check the load. If an insert has the wrong revision, I tell them where to put the affected parts and whom to contact. I identify which order details and photographs will help the reviewer make a decision. I avoid asking the operator to decide whether an unapproved part provides equivalent protection.

I write down any fallback recipe that has already been checked. For example, a large mailer may be allowed for one product if a specified spacer is available. I show the exact part and where to place it. If the spacer is absent, the exception remains on hold. I tell the team how to record the alternate so we can see how often the preferred carton runs out.

I use the [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) to set a consistent photo sequence for exceptions. I ask the team to show the order configuration before changing it. That gives me a way to identify whether the issue comes from the carton, the product or an unclear instruction.

## How do I check that the partner can follow the instruction?

An experienced trainer can make a new process look easy by answering every question. I test the instruction with someone who has not seen the pack before.

**I watch a new operator pack representative orders without coaching. I review the finished parcels against the approved sample, revise unclear steps, and record which version is ready for the first shift.**

### I observe the first run and verify the handoff

I choose a normal order and a combination that uses a different insert or carton. I give the operator the materials and the instruction. I note where they pause, turn a part over or select the wrong piece. I let the written stop conditions guide the trial. If I need to intervene to protect the product, I record where the instruction failed. I check the finished parcel and compare it with the approved reference.

I use the [packaging sample types guide](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to make sure the trial parts represent the materials the partner will receive. If the finished parcel differs from the approved configuration, I check the change before releasing it. I also confirm that the packing station can display the current instruction and identify any printed copy that must be replaced.

I agree on who checks the first production orders and who updates the instruction. I record the revision, approved combinations and effective date. If a supplier changes a part or the warehouse adopts a new closure method, I ask the partner to repeat the affected step before using the new version. That makes the instruction a working reference for later shifts.

## Conclusion

I turn the approved sample into visible steps and stop conditions, then verify that a new operator can reproduce the pack.

[^1]: [Lean Enterprise Institute on standardized work](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/standardized-work/) describes work sequence and standard in-process inventory.
[^2]: [FedEx packing guidance](https://www.fedex.com/en-ca/resources-tools/pack.html) discusses cushioning, sealing and labeling.
