Sourcing & cost

How do I split a packaging MOQ across artwork variants?

By PackHubGlobe Editorial Team · 10 November 2026

I can meet a supplier's total minimum and still fall short on my smallest design. The quote has to say where the minimum applies.

I split a packaging MOQ only after the supplier confirms which materials, structures, and finishes can share a run. I quote each design's required quantity separately and confirm how shortages, excess units, and repeat orders will be handled.

I use one quantity table to compare suppliers and another to record what the factory may actually make. I keep them separate until the production route and quantity limits are clear.

Which minimum should I ask the supplier to confirm?

An order minimum can look like permission to mix designs. It does not tell me whether each design or finish has its own floor.

I ask for the minimum per order, per common structure, and per artwork variant. I ask which production step sets each limit and whether any finish or material must be quoted separately.

I distinguish the structure from the printed version

I first specify the format, board or film, size, and finish. Then I provide a separate row for each artwork file. I make clear that a design change means the graphics change while the agreed construction stays the same. If one carton needs foil and the others do not, I mark it as a separate case. If a pouch needs a different zipper, I do the same. I do not assume either case qualifies for a shared run because the outside dimensions match.

Minimum What I ask the supplier to confirm
Total order Which units count toward the quoted total?
Common structure Which items can use the same material and converting setup?
Artwork version How many usable units must I order for each print file?
Extra finish Does a separate operation introduce another minimum?

I ask the supplier to return the exact proposed split as part of the quote. A reply that says “mixed designs allowed” is incomplete if it does not show the quantities. I also ask whether a separate proof or setup fee applies to every file. That fee may change the economics even when the total unit count is acceptable.

I use the custom packaging MOQ guide to distinguish an order constraint from the other details that determine a quote. If a seller cannot give a number before it receives the finished artwork, I ask which inputs it needs and keep that offer provisional.

How do I compare a shared run with separate runs?

A low shared-run price can leave me with the wrong mix. I compare the usable quantity of each design before comparing the price per unit.

I ask for the finished quantities and total cost under each proposed production route. I compare a shared run and separate runs against the same demand, delivery date, and acceptable excess for each variant.

I test the print plan against a lopsided order

I might want 3,000 units of a standard carton, 1,400 units of a second carton design, and 600 units of a launch edition. Those numbers are illustrative. I ask whether the supplier can make that mix on one production plan and whether the stated quantities refer to saleable finished pieces. I want to know if the supplier will alter the mix to match the way designs fit on a printed sheet. I also ask if one design can be delayed while the other two proceed. That matters when the launch edition still needs approval.

Esko's folding carton planning material describes grouping jobs by factors such as substrate and planning layouts around quantities.1 I use that to frame a question for the supplier, not as proof that any requested combination will work. I ask to see the expected split after the supplier accounts for printing, cutting, and finishing. If the shared plan leaves me with too many units of one carton, I ask for the closest workable option and an exact-quantity alternative.

I also ask for a digital option where the supplier offers it for the chosen format. HP lists a digital press intended for short-run folding cartons.2 I use that as evidence that a different print route is possible in this category. I still confirm material, color and finishing, as well as the complete price. A change of press does not automatically remove minimums downstream.

How do I compare the cost of excess units with a higher print price?

A cheaper unit price looks good on a spreadsheet. It may tie up cash in a design I cannot use after the launch.

I compare total cash spent, expected usable units by design, and the cost of covering any shortfall. I give excess units a value only when I have a realistic use for them.

I keep useful stock separate from extra stock

For the same illustrative demand, suppose one supplier quotes 6,000 pieces at $0.35 each, while another quotes 5,000 at $0.41 each. The quoted printing totals would be $2,100 and $2,050 before other costs. I do not choose from that math alone. I check which design receives the extra thousand units, whether the quoted figures include proofs and freight, and whether the order can meet the ship date. I use the same accounting in both columns of the packaging quote checklist.

An extra thousand of the standard design might replace a later purchase. An extra thousand of the launch carton might have no useful life after the event. I mark those outcomes separately. I may include extra stock in a sensitivity calculation, but I do not call it a saving unless the extra units meet a realistic need. When the chosen mix falls short of one design, I calculate the cost and timing of a replacement run rather than averaging the shortfall into the total.

I also check whether I can replenish the fast seller without ordering the slow seller again. I ask for a separate quote for that reorder. If the savings depend on repeating the initial mix every time, I compare them against a short first run and a larger second run of the confirmed winner. The best purchase can change once I include that second order.

What should I put on the order so the split is enforceable in practice?

A total count can pass while the launch SKU runs short. I need each print version to appear on the order and on the delivered cartons.

I list each variant, file revision, target count, and allowed quantity range as its own order line. I confirm pack labels and ask the supplier to report finished quantities separately before dispatch.

I define the minimum I can actually receive

I distinguish the factory's minimum order from the buyer's minimum usable receipt. If I need 600 of the launch design, I specify that floor and ask the supplier to quote around it. A percentage tolerance that sounds small at order level can be large for one variant. I record whether excess quantities require consent and how they will be priced. I also confirm whether one variant can ship ahead of the rest or whether I need the entire set together.

Before production, I approve each version with its own reference. I use the multi-SKU sample approval process to keep the graphic and quantity decisions tied to the same item. I ask the supplier to label each shipping case with the item code, design revision when relevant, and count. If designs look similar in stacked cartons, I ask for a packing list showing how to find each variant without opening every case.

At dispatch, I compare the reported counts with the separate order lines. If one falls below its floor, I ask for the proposed remedy and timing before treating the order as complete. I retain the final split and quote assumptions for the next order. I can then tell whether a later price change reflects a different mix, a different production route, or a fee that was included the first time.

Conclusion

I buy against the quantity I can use for each design. I confirm the production split and record the exact acceptable counts before placing the order.
