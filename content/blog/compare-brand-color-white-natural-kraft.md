# How do I compare white and natural kraft surfaces for brand color?

I can send the same color reference to two printers and receive a bright mark on white board and a muted one on natural kraft.

**I print the same artwork on the actual white and natural kraft boards, then compare it under agreed lighting. I assess whether the kraft version needs an opaque underprint, another ink treatment, or its own approved color target.**

I start by agreeing which color relationships matter most. Then I ask the printer to make samples that separate the effect of the board from the effect of a change in ink or finish.

## Which samples should I request for a fair comparison?

A printed swatch on white paper cannot show what the same design will look like on the brown board I intend to buy.

**I request both production boards and comparable printed samples. I identify the print method, ink, finish and board side, and ask the printer to record every intentional difference.**

### I keep the test image and the material references together

I ask for the board name, grade, intended print side and the finish on that side. I request unprinted samples alongside the printed ones. I use those to compare the starting colors of the materials. If the kraft board will vary visibly across supply lots, I ask the supplier what range it expects and request representative samples. I use the [packaging materials guide](/guides/packaging-materials-explained/) to make sure the supplier and design team use the same terms for the board and finish.

I put a solid brand color, small text and a few of the actual design elements on the test image. I keep their sizes and positions consistent across the samples. I compare the inks using the intended production method. If the first trial uses a lab drawdown or another printing process, I record that difference and ask what a production proof still needs to confirm.

I inspect both samples after the same preparation and drying interval. I also look at a formed carton if the brand color crosses a fold or meets another panel. A flat proof helps me compare ink coverage. The formed sample helps me see how much of the color is visible on the finished package and whether the board shows at an edge.

## How do I decide whether kraft needs an opaque background?

A pale color can lose contrast against brown board. I check whether a different print construction improves the actual design.

**I compare direct print with an approved opaque underprint or another suitable ink option. I check color, text readability and any visible edges created by the extra layer.**

### I ask for alternatives on the same kraft grade

| Trial | What I compare |
| --- | --- |
| Direct print on white board | Appearance against the approved white reference |
| Direct print on natural kraft | Color shift and readability over the brown surface |
| Print over an opaque underprint | Coverage and alignment at the edge of the color |
| Separate color target for kraft | Whether the adapted design still looks like the same brand |

I ask the printer which options its equipment can reproduce. An underprint may add a pass or change the finish. I look at where it starts and ends, especially beside fine details. If the opaque area extends beyond the intended design, I check whether a light edge becomes visible. I also compare solid areas across several samples so I can see whether coverage is consistent. I document the exact construction rather than asking the supplier to match the first good sample by eye.

Pantone explains that its coated and uncoated references use different paper surfaces and may show different appearances.[^1] I use the reference to communicate the intended color. I still approve the result on the actual board. I use the [packaging print proof guide](/guides/packaging-print-proof/) to record which proof represents the production ink and finish.

## How should I judge the two colors side by side?

One sample can look close under office lights and different next to the product on a shelf. I agree on the comparison conditions first.

**I compare the samples under the agreed viewing conditions and beside the products or packs they will accompany. If I use measurements, I agree on the method and reference for each board.**

### I separate the instrument reading from the visual decision

I put the samples side by side in the orientation in which a buyer will see them. I compare the solid color and the small text. I record the lighting and the angle when surface reflection matters. I ask the brand team to identify which difference would be unacceptable and where an intentional variation is permitted. I use the [guide to brand color across materials](/guides/brand-color-across-materials/) to keep the approval focused on the assembled range as well as the individual box.

If the team uses a color instrument, I ask the printer to agree on the sample location, settings and reference. X-Rite's ColorCert documentation describes configuring color quality workflows.[^2] I use the same agreed setup for samples I intend to compare. I do not treat readings taken under different conditions as equivalent. I ask which metric and limit the team will use before the supplier starts production.

I also look at differences within each board type. If the kraft base varies from sheet to sheet, I check whether the printed result remains acceptable across the expected range. I keep an approved boundary sample if it helps the team make consistent decisions. I record what I saw alongside any measurement rather than using one number to describe gloss, texture and color together.

## What should I record before approving both surfaces?

A supplier can receive one color number for both materials and choose its own compromise. I send a separate reference for each accepted construction.

**I record the approved board, ink construction, finish, viewing method and reference sample for each surface. I identify changes that require a new proof or color check.**

### I make the two approvals easy to distinguish

I name the white board and kraft board separately on the order. I describe the intended ink and whether a separate underprint is included. I attach the artwork revision and sample reference. If the kraft option uses an adapted color, I state where that option is approved. I do not leave the press operator to decide which reference belongs to which sheet.

I ask for the price and setup difference before final approval. If an underprint improves one feature but adds cost, I compare it with the approved adapted design. I check whether the difference applies to every variant or only the ones with very pale artwork. I retain both the visual decision and the purchasing decision so the next order can use the same assumptions.

At receipt, I compare the printed packs with the appropriate references. I check that the supplied board and finish match the record. If the board source, finish or printing process changes, I ask the printer which checks need to be repeated. I retain the previous sample so the brand team can compare the new result with the packs already in use. I also record which face of the carton was measured. The printer can then check the same panel after a reorder instead of choosing an area with a different ink build.

## Conclusion

I compare real proofs on both boards, agree on a reference for each surface, and recheck color when the construction changes.

[^1]: [Pantone guidance on coated and uncoated references](https://support.pantone.com/en/what-does-the-terms-coated-and-uncoated-mean-in-the-pantone-graphics-system) explains how paper surfaces affect appearance.
[^2]: [X-Rite ColorCert configuration guide](https://www.xrite.com/-/media/xrite/files/manuals_and_userguides/c/colorcert/colorcert_startupconfigguide_en.pdf) describes setting up a color quality workflow.
