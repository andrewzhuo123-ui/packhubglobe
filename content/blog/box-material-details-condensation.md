# Which material details matter when a box is exposed to condensation?

I can specify a moisture-resistant face and still find a soft corner where water reached the cut edge.

**I identify where condensation forms and where water collects. I check the board grade, treated faces, cut edges, seams and adhesives, then compare loaded samples through the real cold-to-warm transition and handling sequence.**

I start with the route the carton takes. A damp outer face, water from the primary container and a wet stack at receiving need different checks.

## Where does the water first reach the box?

A damp carton can come from condensation, a leaking container or contact with a wet surface. I record when the first drops appear.

**I follow the package from cold storage to packing, staging and receiving. I photograph the first wet area and record the product temperature, surrounding conditions and position of the box.**

### I trace the path of the water before changing the board

I compare the box before loading, just after loading and after the cold product reaches warmer air. I look at the outside of the primary container, the base of the carton and any divider touching the product. I check whether water runs toward a corner when the case is tilted or stacked. I keep the observation sequence with the sample reference. The [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) helps me record the box and contents before moving them.

I keep condensation and leakage separate in the record. If I find liquid inside the pack, I ask the product team to check the primary container. I also look at what happened around the box. A wet bench or a neighboring carton can transfer water without the box itself being the first place it formed. I photograph the wet area before wiping it so the pattern remains visible.

I note how long the carton stays damp and when it needs to carry weight. A box that dries while sitting alone may behave differently if it is moved or stacked while wet. I ask which stage matters most to the warehouse and include that stage in the comparison. I use actual operating conditions where possible instead of choosing a generic exposure from the carton description.

## Which board and construction details should I ask the supplier for?

A grade name gives me a starting point. It does not identify which surfaces, folds and edges are protected in the finished pack.

**I request the exact board grade and treatments on both faces. I ask how the cut edges, folds and seams are made and identify the adhesives or tapes used at wet contact points.**

### I review the details that remain after converting

| Location | Detail I ask the supplier to identify |
| --- | --- |
| Board face | Grade, treated side and coverage |
| Cut edge | Where exposed board can meet water |
| Fold | Whether the treatment remains continuous after forming |
| Seam and closure | Adhesive or tape and the surface it bonds to |

Holmen's technical information discusses paperboard properties including moisture content and stiffness.[^1] I use the supplier's data to compare grades on a clear basis. I ask what each value describes and whether the sample has the same material and finish as the quote. I use the [packaging materials guide](/guides/packaging-materials-explained/) to keep terms consistent when comparing alternatives.

I also inspect the formed carton. A surface treatment can end at the edge of a blank, and a crease can change the surface seen in a flat sample. I look at the area where water collects. If a glued flap sits at that point, I ask the converter what evidence it has for the joint under the intended conditions. I ask for the material and process that actually made the sample rather than a general description such as moisture resistant. I keep the supplier's construction details with the drawing so a later change can be checked.

## How do I test the box under representative conditions?

A drop of water on a flat sheet tells me little about a closed carton under load. I compare complete packs through the same sequence.

**I load representative cartons, expose them to the intended transition, and check the surfaces, seams and shape while damp. I compare them with a dry control and record the handling and inspection times.**

### I measure the condition when the carton has to work

I start with the normal product and inserts. I compare the carton before exposure with the same design after the expected change in temperature and air conditions. I check the time at which the team would lift, close or stack the carton. I record any soft areas and look for changes in the flap alignment or joint. I also note where the box first starts to lose its shape. That gives the converter a more precise question than asking for a stronger board.

ASTM's conditioning practice describes preparing containers, packages and components for testing under defined atmospheric conditions.[^2] I ask the test provider what conditioning and handling checks suit the actual use. I keep a controlled conditioning result distinct from a trial that reproduces visible condensation on a loaded pack. If the moisture comes from the product's surface, I include the primary package and the normal packing interval in the test plan.

I inspect the dry and exposed packs at the same points. I use the [packaging sample types guide](/guides/packaging-sample-types/) to make sure the trial cartons represent the intended construction. I record any difference in the samples before drawing a conclusion from their performance.

## What changes should I compare before choosing a treated board?

A more resistant face may help when water lands on it. It may do little if water collects beneath the box or reaches an exposed seam.

**I compare a material change with ways to keep water away from the weak point. I approve the option that passes the handling check and meets the cost and construction requirements.**

### I test the correction at the place that failed

If water collects at the base, I look at the way the product and carton are staged before considering a different finish. I may compare a suitable support that keeps the wet primary container away from the board. If drops reach a seam, I ask the converter about changing its position or choosing a suitable bond for the actual surfaces. I check the resulting fit and packing sequence. An additional part can affect the time or space the team needs.

I compare the chosen option with the existing pack under the same exposure. I check the box while it is still damp and after it has had the agreed time to dry. I look for delayed lifting, staining or permanent distortion. If a change adds a coating or liner, I update the materials record and review any statement that depended on the old construction. I also check whether the change affects print or closure.

I keep the approved material, supplier, drawing and trial conditions together. I identify the lot and tell receiving what to inspect if damp cartons arrive. If the route or product temperature changes, I review the affected conditions before relying on the earlier approval.

## Conclusion

I trace the water to its contact point, compare the finished carton under real conditions, and approve the construction that still handles safely.

[^2]: [ASTM conditioning practice for containers and packaging components](https://store.astm.org/d4332-13.html) describes conditioning packaging for tests.
