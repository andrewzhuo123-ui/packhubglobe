# How do I check whether a carton stays closed on the packing bench?

A carton can look closed while I hold the flaps down. I need to know what happens after I take my hands away.

**I close the loaded carton using the normal packing method and inspect the seams, tape ends and locks immediately and before dispatch. I compare empty and loaded samples to separate a packing problem from a closure problem.**

I keep the first sample as evidence of how the carton closure behaves. The location of a gap or lifted edge helps me decide whether to change the carton, its contents, or the way it is closed.

## What should I check before applying tape or engaging the lock?

Tape can hide a flap that is under pressure. I look at how the carton closes with the real contents in place.

**I compare an empty carton with a normally loaded carton. I check whether the flaps meet, the tabs line up, and the contents stay below the closure plane.**

### I watch what changes when I add the load

I form the carton using the normal sequence. I check that the corners are seated and that the bottom is properly assembled. I close the empty sample gently and note where the flaps meet. Then I pack the product, insert, wrap and paperwork. I check the same points again. If the gap appears only with the load inside, I look for the highest part of the pack. It might be a folded leaflet, an overlapping pad, or a product that has moved out of its recess.

I do not push a bulging carton flat to make the test look better. I note how much effort the packer uses and whether the flap rises when released. I use the [guide to internal and external box dimensions](/guides/internal-vs-external-box-dimensions/) to compare the available space with the drawing. A nominal height can be correct while an inner fold or stacked pad reduces usable clearance.

If the empty carton also has a gap, I compare it with an accepted sample. I inspect the crease and any skew at the joint. I check cartons from different bundles if I suspect an assembly or supply variation. I ask the converter to review the affected part using the carton revision and photos. I retain the loaded sample so I can repeat the check once the carton or packing layout changes.

## How do I check whether the tape is holding?

A strip can look smooth at first and lift at the ends while the parcel waits for collection. I inspect it at both points.

**I use the specified tape on representative board, follow its application instructions, and check the ends and seams for lift. I record the carton surface and conditions when I see a defect.**

### I reproduce the tape application used in production

I use the same dispenser and tape the team uses during a shift. I apply the strip to the specified seam and check that it contacts both surfaces along the joint. I look for folds, bubbles, dust and areas where the tape bridges a gap. I compare the application conditions with the tape supplier's instructions. For example, 3M provides product-specific guidance in its data sheet for Scotch Box Sealing Tape 311.[^1] I check the guidance for the exact tape we buy before changing the process.

I check the finished carton immediately and again at the normal dispatch stage. If the tape lifted, I record the location and whether the board surface came away with it. I inspect both sides of the seam to see whether the flap pulled open or the tape released from a flat surface. I also compare cartons taken from different points in storage when the temperature or board surface varies.

FedEx describes the H taping method in its packing guidance.[^2] I check the applicable carrier instructions for my shipment and use the specified pattern during the trial. I keep that pattern consistent while comparing tape or board options. A change that only works with more tape or a slower packing process needs to be reflected in the operating instructions and cost comparison.

## How do I check a tuck tab or locking closure?

A tab can disappear under a flap without fully engaging. I need a way to show the packer what a seated lock looks like.

**I check that the lock reaches its intended position and stays there during ordinary bench handling. I verify the result on a loaded carton without adding pressure to force the tab into place.**

### I compare the lock with an accepted sample

I fold the carton in the intended order. I look at the point where the tab enters and check whether it bends, catches, or slips beside the opening. I use the [guide to straight and reverse tuck ends](/guides/straight-vs-reverse-tuck-end/) to describe which flap and orientation I am inspecting. I keep a correctly closed sample at the station when a new team needs to learn the design.

| What I see | What I check next |
| --- | --- |
| Tab pops out after I release the lid | Product height and whether the lock was fully seated |
| Lock catches only on one side | Carton squareness, fold sequence and slot alignment |
| Flap edge is torn after closing | Insertion path and pressure needed to engage the lock |
| Empty carton closes but loaded carton fails | Pack layout and clearance beneath the flap |

I pick up the loaded sample the same way a packer transfers it from the bench to a staging area. I check whether the closure shifts during that motion. If a lock opens, I document the angle and location before trying again. I distinguish a missed step from a design that gives the packer no clear sign of engagement.

I also check whether the pack requires additional sealing for its route. A lock that works during bench handling may still need the sealing method specified for the shipping configuration. I keep that requirement in the packing instruction.

## What result tells me to stop and correct the packing method?

A carton that stays shut once can still fail repeatedly on a different shift. I define a visible pass condition and record exceptions.

**I hold the affected cartons when a seam lifts, a lock releases or the lid bulges beyond the agreed condition. I reproduce the defect, correct the cause, and repeat the check with representative orders.**

### I keep evidence that points to the next change

I photograph the carton before pressing the tape down or reseating the lock. I record the item, box revision, closure material, packing time and where the fault appeared. I also record the load and any unusual assembly step. The [warehouse damage inspection guide](/blog/3pl-damage-inspection-record/) gives me a useful sequence for keeping the condition of the parcel and its contents visible in the record.

I compare the failed pack with an accepted one and change one factor at a time where possible. If removing a folded pad makes the flaps meet, I still verify that the revised pad protects the product. If changing the tape fixes lifting, I repeat the trial with the board and conditions the warehouse actually uses. I ask the packer to reproduce the accepted method without coaching so I can see whether the instruction is clear.

I keep the bench check as an operating check and use suitable distribution testing when I need to evaluate the complete shipping pack. Once the method is approved, I write down the allowed closure materials, the steps to follow, and the visible defects that require a hold. The next shift can then compare its result with the same sample and criteria.

## Conclusion

I check closure with the real load, document where it fails, and verify the corrected method before the pack returns to normal dispatch.
