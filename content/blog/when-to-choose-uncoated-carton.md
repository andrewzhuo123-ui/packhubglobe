# When is an uncoated carton a sensible choice for my product?

I can choose an uncoated carton for its feel and discover that the print looks different once I see the actual board.

**I choose an uncoated carton when its print, fit and handling performance meet the product's requirements without a surface treatment. I check the exact board and production finish using a printed, formed sample and the real product.**

I start by asking what the surface needs to do. The answer helps me compare a plain board with a finish applied for a specific purpose.

## What do I mean when I ask for an uncoated carton?

A supplier can offer an uncoated-looking box that still carries a protective varnish. I ask it to describe the sheet and the finished carton separately.

**I specify which faces of the board are uncoated and whether the converter adds varnish, film or another treatment. I record the actual grade and construction on the sample request.**

### I check the board and the converting steps

I ask for the board grade and the finish on both sides. If the exterior looks natural and the inside uses another surface, I record both. I also ask whether the converter intends to apply a treatment after printing. An uncoated sheet and a finished carton with no added coating are different purchasing descriptions. I use the [packaging materials guide](/guides/packaging-materials-explained/) to keep the description clear for the designer and buyer.

I write down why I am considering the surface. If the team wants a tactile finish, I ask for a sample it can handle. If it wants a softer print appearance, I ask for a proof that shows that effect. If the supplier quotes a different stock to reduce cost, I compare thickness and fit as well as the finish. I identify the exact version on the drawing or quote rather than using a general phrase such as natural paper.

I keep separate notes for the side that touches the product and the side that faces the customer. A carton may need a different inside surface if the product can transfer moisture or oil. I ask the quality team to identify the contact conditions and the requirements it must verify before the supplier changes any part of the construction.

## How do I know whether my design will print clearly?

A color match on a screen does not tell me how ink will look on the chosen sheet. I ask for a representative printed sample.

**I check the artwork on the actual board, including solid color, fine details and small text. I inspect a formed sample before deciding whether the print needs an adjustment or a different surface.**

### I compare the areas that matter to the reader

I give the printer the actual design and identify the parts that must remain clear. I point out small light text, dark solids and the brand colors that appear beside another component. I ask how the ink and process will interact with the proposed board. Holmen's paperboard guidance discusses ink absorption and drying.[^1] I use the printer's recommendation and the actual proof to assess the proposed construction.

| Feature | What I check on the proof |
| --- | --- |
| Solid color | Evenness and appearance under agreed lighting |
| Small text | Open letter shapes and readable contrast |
| Folded edge | Cracking or a visible change in the printed area |
| Surface in hand | Feel, visible rub marks and color transfer |

I use the [packaging print proof guide](/guides/packaging-print-proof/) to record the board, print method and finish. I inspect the sample after folding because a sharp edge or large crease can change how the panel looks. I compare it with the intended product and any adjoining sleeve or label. If the print misses one requirement, I ask whether changing the design, ink or stock would solve it. I retest the part that caused the failure instead of assuming every uncoated option will behave the same way.

## What handling and contact conditions should I check?

A clean sample can look good in a meeting and pick up marks on the packing line. I check the carton where it will be handled.

**I load the real product and check the carton after packing, storage and representative handling. I inspect scuffing, stains, closure and any surface that touches the product.**

### I recreate the sequence that can change the surface

I use the intended packing materials and follow the order the team uses to assemble the carton. I note where the product or an insert touches the inside. I check the carton before and after placing it in its shipping case. If the outer faces rub against one another, I document the pattern. I also look at the way the warehouse takes the boxes out. A narrow loading path can leave a mark before transport begins.

I consider the conditions the product brings into the pack. A chilled product may need a check for moisture on the outside of its primary container. An oily product can raise a different question if it can reach the board. I confirm the applicable contact requirements with the product team and use approved samples in the trial. I check the actual carton rather than assuming a print coating will serve as the required protection.

I use the [guide to protective inserts](/blog/fragile-product-inserts-transit-testing/) when the mark follows an insert or neighboring product. I check whether a change in fit or a different separator solves the issue. If the carton needs a treatment after those checks, I document the surface and performance I need the treatment to provide.

## When is a coated or treated version worth the change?

A treatment can improve one property while changing cost, appearance or the way the pack should be assessed after use.

**I add a treatment when a representative trial shows that it resolves a defined problem. I compare the complete construction, packing work and supplier evidence before approving it.**

### I make the decision from paired samples

I ask for a plain and a treated version with comparable geometry and artwork. I check whether the treatment changes the board or another part of the specification. I repeat the print, fold and handling checks that matter to the job. I compare the accepted results at the same order quantity and delivery terms. I record any extra setup, minimum or lead time so the buyer can see what the improvement costs.

I also check the material description before changing any public claim. The 4evergreen circularity guidance discusses barriers, coatings and treatments in paper-based packaging design.[^2] I review the actual construction and the guidance for the sales market. I avoid inferring a disposal route from the carton surface alone. I retain the supplier's material information and document which version the evidence covers.

If the uncoated sample meets the requirements, I specify the accepted grade and print construction on the order. If I choose a treatment, I name its purpose, side and coverage. I keep a formed reference sample and identify which changes require another check. That makes the decision usable for the next buyer and the receiving team. I also record where the supplier may apply glue or tape. If that area changes with a new finish, I check the bond on the formed carton before approving the order.

## Conclusion

I choose the simplest carton surface that passes the actual print, fit and handling requirements, and retain the sample that supports the choice.

[^2]: [4evergreen Circularity by Design guideline](https://4evergreenforum.eu/about/circularity-by-design-guideline/) discusses construction choices for paper-based packaging.
